:

ταῦτα δὲ αὐτοῦ ἐνθυμηθέντος ἰδοὺ ἄγγελος κυρίου κατ’ ὄναρ ἐφάνη αὐτῷ λέγων· Ἰωσὴφ υἱὸς Δαυίδ, μὴ φοβηθῇς παραλαβεῖν Μαρίαν τὴν γυναῖκά σου, τὸ γὰρ ἐν αὐτῇ γεννηθὲν ἐκ πνεύματός ἐστιν ἁγίου·

But as he was pondering these things, behold, angel of kuriosa appeared to him in a dream, saying, "Joseph, son of David, do not be afraid to paralambanceb Mary your wife, for that which was genniedc in her is from holy pneumad."

Notes

a
kuriou κύριος ranges from an ordinary term of respectful address ("sir," "master") through a disciple's title for a living teacher to the full divine name. In the Septuagint it is the standard reverential substitute for the unpronounced Hebrew Tetragrammaton. When the New Testament quotes an Old Testament passage that names it, the Greek text carries the same word, κύριος, with no orthographic marker distinguishing it from the word's ordinary human sense.↑
Usually lord, master, sir
Sources
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — states κύριος is used "in LXX (subst.) chiefly for [the divine name], also for בַּעַל, אָדוֹן," cataloguing the full range from genitive-of-possession ("master of the house") through vocative address ("sir") to the full divine title
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — "supreme in authority, i.e. controller; by implication, Master (as a respectful title)"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — cautions against over-reading an anarthrous κύριος as inherently a divine title, noting Psalm 110:1's "my lord" is ordinary court address to a king
  • France (NICNT) — on Matthew's citation of Isaiah 40:3, applying to Jesus a text whose κύριος names God himself constitutes "a remarkable christological claim"
  • Hengel (Studies in Early Christology) — traces how Psalm 110:1's double κύριος became the primary vehicle for the earliest church's confession of Jesus's exalted status
b
paralabein παραλαμβάνω is an ordinary verb for taking a person along or to oneself, used elsewhere in Matthew for taking children on a journey, taking companions up a mountain, and marching a prisoner into custody. Applied to a wife, it marks the second stage of Jewish marriage law, bringing a betrothed woman into the groom's household. No lexicon entry treats the marital use as a distinct sense apart from the verb's other objects.↑
Usually taken, received, took unto
Sources
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — groups Matthew 1:20's "take as wife" in the same sense as taking companions up a mountain or a prisoner into custody
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — cites Herodotus, two passages of Josephus, and the rabbinic term for bringing a bride into the groom's house
  • Albright and Mann (Anchor) — describes betrothal and the taking of the bride into the groom's house as the two legal stages of Jewish marriage
  • France (NICNT) — reads the verb as carrying a sense of welcome, not mere dutiful compliance
c
gennēthen γεννηθέν is the neuter passive participle of γεννάω, the same verb behind 1:16's ἐγεννήθη. The article τό substantizes the participle into an unspecified subject, an ordinary construction that recurs in Matthew's own fulfillment formulas. The neuter form leaves the child's gender undisclosed until the following verse.↑
Usually begat, born, brought forth
Sources
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — reads the neuter construction as withholding the child's gender until verse 21, and the intervening ἐστιν as blocking a reading of divine paternity
  • Blass, Debrunner and Funk (A Greek Grammar of the New Testament) — classifies the construction as an ordinary neuter substantival participle, citing Matthew's own "to rhēthen" as a parallel
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — argue Greek's neuter πνεῦμα, unlike its feminine Hebrew counterpart, tells against reading the Spirit's role as a male parent
  • France (NICNT) — notes "pregnant from" is unnatural English idiom while "pregnant by" wrongly implies natural male generation
d
pneumatos Pneuma spans a range from ordinary wind or breath to the animating principle in a person, an incorporeal spirit or demon, and the divine Spirit. Greek grammar does not resolve which sense is meant in a given case. The presence or absence of the article is sometimes read as marking the divine Spirit specifically, though this pattern is not applied consistently and remains disputed.↑
Usually spirit, ghost, wind
Sources
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — base sense "a current of air, i.e. breath (blast) or a breeze," extending by analogy to human soul, angel, demon, or God's own Spirit
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — five-sense entry, running from wind and breath through vital principle, disposition and incorporeal being to Holy Spirit, where the article's presence "as a rule" marks the Spirit as Person/Divine Power versus an anarthrous "operation, influence, or gift"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) and Davies and Allison (ICC) — both argue, independently, that "ἐκ πνεύματος ἁγίου" denotes divine creative power rather than a paternal/male generative role, Nolland via Septuagint idiom, Davies and Allison via grammatical gender (Greek πνεῦμα is neuter, Hebrew רוּחַ is feminine)
  • SBL Greek New Testament — πνεῦμα ἅγιον is anarthrous at Matthew 1:18, 1:20, and 3:11 but articular at 12:32 and 28:19, a distributional pattern whose significance remains disputed absent a dedicated grammar treatment
