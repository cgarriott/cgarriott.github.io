Notes

a
Biblos βίβλος names a book or a scroll. It occurs ten times in the New Testament and only here in Matthew, and in 41 of its 43 Septuagint occurrences it means a book, most often a book of Scripture. That usage is the ground for reading the heading over the whole Gospel. Against it stands the Septuagint's own use of the word for a document included within a larger work, which leaves the genealogy alone, the birth narrative, and the two infancy chapters all in play.↑
Usually book
Sources
  • Boring (The New Interpreter's Bible) — supplies the Septuagint count, and concludes that "This suggests that 1:1 does not designate merely the genealogy, or Jesus' birth story, but is Matthew's title for his whole work"
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — since no shorter unit could plausibly be labelled a book, the heading reaches the Gospel as a whole
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — the same conclusion, on the same ground, a book's first word pointing to the book
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — "It is considerably more natural (given the link here with 'origin') to apply βίβλος to the genealogy bracketed by vv. 1 and 17"
  • Gundry (Matthew: A Commentary on His Literary and Theological Art) and Hagner (WBC) — also confine the heading to the genealogy, Hagner on the strength of the frame at verses 1 and 17
  • Brown (The Birth of the Messiah) — confines the heading to the genealogy, admits 1:18-25 only so far as it explains 1:16, and calls any stretch to the whole Gospel "fanciful"
  • Carson (EBC) and Blomberg (NAC) — a heading for the first two chapters, Blomberg on the ground that they form the narrative's natural unit
  • Turner (BECNT) — the genealogy together with the birth narrative, on Fenton's telescopic image of a title that reaches further as the book goes on
  • France (NICNT) — the whole-Gospel reading puts "too much weight on the term 'book'"
b
geneseōs Βίβλος γενέσεως repeats a rare phrase from the Greek Septuagint's own Genesis 2:4 and 5:1, both introducing accounts of a beginning. Commentators disagree on how much weight that echo carries, from a light stylistic flourish to a claim that the Gospel opens as a new act of creation. They also disagree on how far the resulting title reaches, from the seventeen-verse genealogy alone to the entire Gospel.↑
Usually birth, natural
Sources
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — reads the phrase as a deliberate "new genesis," extending the title to the whole Gospel
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — reads the echo as real but rhetorically modest, and confines the title to the genealogy alone
  • Hagner (WBC) — treats the echo as carrying near-maximal theological weight while still limiting the title's reach to the genealogy
  • Boring (The New Interpreter's Bible) — argues from the Septuagint's own frequent use of "book" that the title covers the whole Gospel
  • Goulder (Midrash and Lection in Matthew) — reads the echo as a lectionary marker, tying the Gospel's opening to Genesis's place in the synagogue reading cycle
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — holds that as the book's first word "βίβλος quite clearly indicates that the following title refers to the entire book," while noting that γένεσις pulls the other way and leaves "interpreters with a dilemma"
c
christou This occurrence opens a chiasm with the same word at 1:17, bounding the genealogy between two uses of the title. Most readings treat every occurrence in the surrounding chapters as titular. Two dissent. One counts this occurrence among four, out of roughly eighteen in the Gospel, that are not exclusively titular. The other reads the opening pair as a double name. Χριστός is ambiguous between a title, the Anointed One or Messiah, and a fixed second name for Jesus. Scholars disagree on where the word tips from title into name, and some read it as doing both at once. The verb χρίω behind it means to anoint, so every anointing in the New Testament is the same word as the name. Χριστιανός is the partisan noun built on it, formed with a Latin suffix meaning someone's people, as the Herodians were Herod's. It occurs three times, and what it meant at Antioch is disputed.↑
Usually christ
Sources
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — titular here, the chiasm formed by verses 1 and 17 favouring it
  • France (NICNT) — with Nolland, reading the opening as a title rather than a name
  • Carson (EBC) — the outlier, whose own tally makes this occurrence one of the few not exclusively titular
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — the second dissent, taking the opening pair as "probably a double name", since Matthew as a rule articulates the word where it is a title
  • Matthew 1:17 — the other end of the frame, where the word recurs articular
d
adelphous Isaac's brother Ishmael and Jacob's brother Esau go unnamed at the parallel points earlier in the same sentence. Commentators read the asymmetry as a deliberate signal, most often tracing it to the twelve tribes of Israel through Judah's reception of the royal scepter in Genesis 49:10. ἀδελφός is a brother by birth. It also covers wider kin, a compatriot, and a member of a religious association. The Greek Septuagint calls Abraham and his nephew Lot adelphoi. Whether that range reaches cousin in the New Testament is disputed, and the men named as Jesus's adelphoi are the case in question. Greek had a separate word for cousin and the New Testament uses it once. The masculine plural can take in women, and the versions divide over printing sisters.↑
Usually brother
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — reads the phrase as "an allusion to the twelve tribes who form the locus of the people of God and the pattern for the twelve apostles"
  • Gundry (Matthew: A Commentary on His Literary and Theological Art) — traces it instead to an interest in the people of God as a brotherhood, where "Judah and his brothers prefigure the brotherhood of the church"
  • Hagner (WBC) — reads it as pointing to the twelve tribes of Israel, with Jesus as the culmination that carries the genealogy's theological fullness back to all Israel
  • Hood (The Messiah, His Brothers, and the Nations), cited in Rieske (Themelios) — reads it as foreshadowing Jesus's own self-sacrifice on behalf of his brothers
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — finds the reference "understandable" since Jacob's other sons are the ancestors of the twelve tribes

Aram Matthew's Greek reads Ἀράμ, the same spelling as the Septuagint's text of 1 Chronicles 2:9-10. The Septuagint itself is inconsistent: the same figure is spelled Ἀρραν at Ruth 4:19, and both spellings differ from the Hebrew form behind them, Ram. Translations split on which name to print: some transliterate Matthew's own wording as "Aram," others harmonize to the familiar Hebrew "Ram."↑
Usually ram
Sources
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — derives Ἀράμ from the Hebrew and glosses it "Aram (i.e. Ram), an Israelite"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — traces the double spelling to an internal doublet in the Septuagint's own text of 1 Chronicles 2:9–10
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — independently confirm the same Septuagint-internal mechanism from the primary texts
  • Wasserman (Evangelical Textual Criticism) — notes that confidence in the parallel reading at Luke 3:33 has since weakened in a later critical edition

Asaph The earliest recoverable Greek text reads Asaph, the Levitical psalmist, where the genealogy requires Asa, king of Judah. The two names come from different Hebrew roots and are not variant spellings of the same word. Manuscript evidence favors Asaph strongly enough that most textual critics treat it as the original reading.↑
Sources
  • Metzger (A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament) — rates the Asaph reading "almost certain"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — calls it "a deliberate ploy to evoke yet more of the history of the people of God"
  • Carson (EBC) — rejects the deliberate-allusion theory as "too cryptic to be believable," pointing to looser ancient orthography
  • Keener (A Commentary on the Gospel of Matthew) — treats the allusion as a known Jewish exegetical technique, not an idiosyncratic move

Amōs The earliest recoverable Greek text reads Amos, the eighth-century prophet, where the genealogy requires Amon, king of Judah and father of Josiah. Unlike the parallel case at Matthew 1:7, Amos and Amon come from unrelated Hebrew names rather than variant spellings of one word. Manuscript evidence favors Amos strongly enough that most textual critics treat it as the original reading.↑
Sources

  • Nolland (NIGTC) — finds a deliberate allusion "much less certain" here than at the parallel Asaph case, since Amos is "well attested as an LXX reading for Amon"
  • France (NICNT) — reads the manuscripts of Chronicles that name the king Amos as "likely to be due to the influence of Matthew"
  • Turner (BECNT) — treats the same manuscript evidence as a source the genealogy inherited, not a consequence of it

k
Iechonian Matthew's clause moves directly from Josiah to Jeconiah, omitting Jehoiakim, the king named as the intervening generation in 1 Chronicles 3:15-16. Greek manuscripts of the Old Testament sometimes spell Jehoiakim and Jehoiachin (Jeconiah) identically, a documented confusion that may account for the compression. Whether the missing generation is an inherited textual accident or a deliberate compositional device remains disputed.↑

metoikesias μετοικεσία is a term of the Greek Old Testament for the Babylonian captivity, and the New Testament uses it only here and at 1:12 and 1:17. At 2 Kings 24:16 it names the deportation of seven thousand men of valor, the event in which Jeconiah himself was carried to Babylon. Commentators and study Bibles divide over which deportation the phrase marks, that one or the destruction of the temple a decade later.↑
Usually carrying away
Sources

Iechonias The king appears in the Old Testament under three names: Jehoiachin in Kings, Coniah in Jeremiah, and Jeconiah in the genealogy of 1 Chronicles 3, which is the form Matthew's Greek takes up. English versions divide over the spelling, some keeping the Hebrew-derived Jeconiah and others transliterating the Greek as Jechoniah. Jeremiah 22:30 records the same king as childless and bars his offspring from David's throne. The clause here names his son.↑
Usually jeconiah, jechonias
Sources
  • France (NICNT) — takes the name from the Greek and Hebrew of 1 Chronicles 3:16-17, and judges that "it was probably his given name, Jehoiachin being a throne name"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — reports that the Greek Old Testament drops two of the three forms, so that "'Jehoiachin' is either 'Jechoniah' or (a second) 'Jehoiakim'"
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — derives the Greek name from the Hebrew form rendered Jeconiah in the Old Testament
  • MacArthur Study Bible — reports that "[a] curse on him forbade any of his descendants from the throne of David forever," and holds that Jesus escapes it through a legal rather than physical descent
  • Gundry (Matthew: A Commentary on His Literary and Theological Art) — limits the curse to Jeconiah's own sons, since "the Lord called his grandson Zerubbabel a chosen 'signet'"

Salathiēl The Hebrew text of 1 Chronicles 3:19 names Zerubbabel as the son of Pedaiah, not Shealtiel (Salathiel). Every other biblical reference to Zerubbabel, including the Septuagint's own rendering of that same verse, calls him son of Shealtiel. Proposed explanations include a levirate marriage, a shift in family headship after Shealtiel died without an heir, and a substitution driven by the curse pronounced on Jeconiah's own offspring in Jeremiah 22.↑
Usually shealtiel
Sources
  • Gundry (Matthew: A Commentary on His Literary and Theological Art) — proposes that the curse on Jeconiah's line led to "replacement of his son Pedaiah with Salathiel, who came from another branch of David's family"
  • Turner (BECNT) — suggests Pedaiah "entered a levirate marriage with Shealtiel's widow to raise up children for his brother"
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — Pedaiah the actual father, with Zerubbabel called Shealtiel's son because he succeeded him as head of the family
  • Albright and Mann (Anchor) — reads the discrepancy as a confusion already present in the Greek textual tradition, with Zerubbabel's identity merged with a cousin's
  • Gill (Exposition of the Old and New Testament), known through secondary digests — reports an alternate reading, attributed to the medieval exegete Kimchi, in which Zerubbabel counts as Shealtiel's grandson rather than his son

egennēsen The active egennēsen runs down the genealogy thirty-nine times, and this is the last of them. Its object is Joseph, and the birth of Jesus in the same sentence takes the passive. What the verb claims of each pair it links is disputed. Two lexicons cite the genealogy itself as their example of the plain procreative sense, though the list skips three reigning kings between Joram and Uzziah at 1:8. Commentators read it as fathering, as descent at a remove, and as legal inheritance.↑
Usually begat, born, brought forth
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — counts the active thirty-nine times before the shift, and holds that where names are dropped the verb is "to be understood as 'was the ancestor of,' not 'was the father of'"
  • France (NICNT) — the verb "is properly used of the male role in procreation, though in a formal genealogy its repetition apparently allows a less immediate relationship, including a gap of three generations in v. 8"
  • Machen (The Virgin Birth of Christ) — reads it as a legal claim throughout, since "[t]he word 'begat' in the genealogy means simply 'had as a legal heir'"
  • Danker (A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament) — files the genealogy's whole run under becoming a parent by procreation, and compares a Greek genealogy in Diodorus Siculus that repeats the same verb the same way
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — cites Matthew 1:1-16 as its own example of the active used of the father, "to beget"
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — calls it "[t]he key verb of the genealogy" and marks its return at 2:1

dekatessares The Hebrew consonants of David's name sum to fourteen. Whether the genealogy's three fourteens carry that arithmetic, mark a providential ordering of Israel's history, serve as an aid to memory, or follow from the names the list leaves out, is disputed. The count itself does not come out. The names of the preceding verses total fourteen, fourteen and thirteen unless Jeconiah is counted in two sections while David is counted in one.↑
Usually fourteen
Sources
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — argue the case from the numerical value of David's name, citing the Greek Old Testament's own Genesis 46 as precedent for an unflagged gematria in a genealogy, and settle on it as one factor rather than the sole one
  • Nolland (NIGTC), Turner (BECNT), Carson (EBC), Gundry (Matthew: A Commentary on His Literary and Theological Art) and Mitch and Sri (CCSS) — take the derivation from David's name as established
  • France (NICNT) and Hagner (WBC) — object that the arithmetic would demand a Hebrew literacy a Greek readership is unlikely to have had, and that the text draws no explicit link
  • Albright and Mann (Anchor) — reject every numerological reading of the list, the value of David's name included, as the work of a "desk mind" rather than verifiable historical research
  • Brown (The Birth of the Messiah) — has Matthew combining two inherited lists that already ran to fourteen, and treats the name arithmetic as a catalyst rather than a plan
  • Goulder (Midrash and Lection in Matthew) — derives the figure instead from a rabbinic reading of the monarchy as a waxing and waning moon, with the exile as its dark and Jesus as a second full moon
  • Blomberg (NAC) and Keener (A Commentary on the Gospel of Matthew) — hold that ancient counting alternated between inclusive and exclusive reckoning, so the totals need no repair
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — finds that "the arrangement given in v. 17 does not add up", and reads the summary as applied to a genealogy Matthew received
  • MacArthur Study Bible — "[t]he significance of the number 14 is not clear", and offers the ordering as an aid to memorization
  • Ehrman (The Bart Ehrman Blog) — reads the shortfall as an error rather than a device

genesis Two disputes attach to this single word. Textually, the Textus Receptus and the Byzantine tradition read "gennēsis" ("birth") where every other collated edition reads "genesis" ("origin, becoming"), a substitution attributed to scribes narrowing an unfamiliar broader sense toward a more specialized one. kuriou κύριος ranges from an ordinary term of respectful address ("sir," "master") through a disciple's title for a living teacher to the full divine name. In the Septuagint it is the standard reverential substitute for the unpronounced Hebrew Tetragrammaton. When the New Testament quotes an Old Testament passage that names it, the Greek text carries the same word, κύριος, with no orthographic marker distinguishing it from the word's ordinary human sense.↑
Usually lord, master, sir
Sources
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — states κύριος is used "in LXX (subst.) chiefly for [the divine name], also for [Baal, Adon]," cataloguing the full range from genitive-of-possession ("master of the house") through vocative address ("sir") to the full divine title
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — "supreme in authority, i.e. (as noun) controller; by implication, Master (as a respectful title)"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — cautions against over-reading an anarthrous κύριος as inherently a divine title, noting Psalm 110:1's "my lord" is ordinary court address to a king
  • France (NICNT) — on Matthew's citation of Isaiah 40:3, applying to Jesus a text whose κύριος names God himself constitutes "a remarkable christological claim"

eginōsken The verb carries a well-attested sense, "to have relations with," used the same way for the same verb three times in Genesis 4. The imperfect tense describes the state lasting up to the birth. A minority reading takes the imperfect's own aspect as suggesting relations began only afterward, a reading directly rejected elsewhere in the commentary literature.↑

heōs hou "Until" marks the point up to which Joseph and Mary had no relations. Whether the construction says anything about what followed is disputed. Readings range from a plain "no implication either way," through a translation choice meant to sidestep the question, to a claim that ordinary relations resumed once the stated point was reached.↑
Sources
  • Mitch and Sri (CCSS) — read the grammar as settling nothing either way, leaving Catholic doctrine on the question grounded elsewhere
  • France (NICNT) — reads "until" as most naturally implying resumption of ordinary marital relations after the birth
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — translates the word "before" rather than "until" specifically to avoid implying anything about what came after
  • Wright ("Prepositions and Perpetual Virginity: Until as a Scalar Item in Matt 1:25," BAGL) — argues on formal-semantic grounds that the construction carries an obligatory inference of resumption, against the wider scholarly consensus
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — grants that "until" does not necessarily imply a change afterward, while judging perpetual virginity foreign enough to Matthew's own readers that he would have had to state it outright
