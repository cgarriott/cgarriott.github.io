Notes

a
geneseōs Βίβλος γενέσεως repeats a rare phrase from the Greek Septuagint's own Genesis 2:4 and 5:1, both introducing accounts of a beginning. Commentators disagree on how much weight that echo carries, from a light stylistic flourish to a claim that the Gospel opens as a new act of creation. They also disagree on how far the resulting title reaches, from the seventeen-verse genealogy alone to the entire Gospel.↑
Usually birth
Sources
  • Davies & Allison (ICC) — reads the phrase as a deliberate "new Genesis," extending the title to the whole Gospel
  • John Nolland (NIGTC) — reads the echo as real but rhetorically modest, and confines the title to the genealogy alone
  • Donald A. Hagner (WBC) — treats the echo as carrying near-maximal theological weight while still limiting the title's reach to the genealogy
  • M. Eugene Boring (New Interpreter's Bible) — argues from the Septuagint's own frequent use of "book" that the title covers the whole Gospel
  • M.D. Goulder (Midrash and Lection in Matthew) — reads the echo as a lectionary marker, tying the Gospel's opening to Genesis's place in the synagogue reading cycle
b
christou Χριστός is ambiguous between a title -- the Anointed One, the Messiah -- and a fixed second name for Jesus. SBLGNT prints the word lowercase throughout, and STEPBible's TAGNT apparatus classifies it as titular rather than as a personal name, like kyrios. Scholars disagree on where the word tips from title into name, and some read it as doing both at once. CNT renders it "the Anointed" so the title stays audible. The whole family follows from it. χρίω is the verb it is built on, so an anointing anywhere in the New Testament is the same word as the name; χρῖσμα is the substance. Χριστιανός, rendered here "Anointedian", is the partisan noun -- the -ιανός suffix is a Latin formation meaning someone's people, as the Herodians were Herod's. It occurs only three times, and what it meant at Antioch is disputed: an outsiders' nickname, a Roman administrative label, or something the group called itself. "Christian" now carries two thousand years the word did not have then, and it also hides that the word is built on Χριστός at all.↑
Usually christ, jesus
Sources
  • BDAG (Bauer/Danker) — s.v. Χριστός, two senses: titular "Anointed One/Messiah" vs. a personal name; names Matthew 11:2 as roughly the hinge point where sense shifts

adelphous Isaac's brother Ishmael and Jacob's brother Esau go unnamed at the parallel points earlier in the same sentence. Commentators read the asymmetry as a deliberate signal, most often tracing it to the twelve tribes of Israel through Judah's reception of the royal scepter in Genesis 49:10.↑
Usually brother
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — reads the phrase as an allusion to the twelve tribes, the locus of the people of God and the pattern for the twelve apostles
  • Gundry (Matthew: A Commentary on His Literary and Theological Art) — reads it instead as expressing an interest in the people of God as a brotherhood, prefiguring the church
  • Hagner (WBC) — reads it as pointing to the twelve tribes of Israel, whose theological fullness comes to bear on Jesus as their culmination
  • Hood (The Messiah, His Brothers, and the Nations) — reads it as foreshadowing Jesus's own self-sacrifice on behalf of his brothers

Aram Matthew's Greek reads Ἀράμ, the same spelling as the Septuagint's text of 1 Chronicles 2:9–10. The Septuagint itself is inconsistent: the same figure is spelled Ἀρραν at Ruth 4:19, and both spellings differ from the Hebrew form behind them, Ram. Translations split on which name to print: some transliterate Matthew's own wording as "Aram," others harmonize to the familiar Hebrew "Ram."↑
Usually ram
Sources
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary (G0689) — glosses Ἀράμ as "of Hebrew origin; Aram (i.e. Ram)"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — traces the double spelling to an internal doublet in the Septuagint's own text of 1 Chronicles 2:9–10
  • Davies & Allison (ICC) — independently confirm the same Septuagint-internal mechanism from the primary texts
  • Wasserman (Evangelical Textual Criticism blog) — notes that confidence in the parallel reading at Luke 3:33 has since weakened in a later UBS edition
e
basilea David alone among the genealogy's named figures carries a title. Scholars read this as marking a structural pivot in the list, closing its first section before the pattern shifts toward decline and a later climax. A minority view treats the title mainly as standard Old Testament phrasing for David rather than a deliberate structural marker.↑
Usually king
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — reads the title as a deliberate addition beyond Matthew's source text, anticipating Jesus's own royal title
  • Hagner (WBC) — reads it as strengthening the link between David and Jesus as the Davidic, messianic king
  • Gundry (Matthew: A Commentary on His Literary and Theological Art) — reads it as marking the close of the genealogy's first section and casting David as a prototype of Jesus
  • Albright & Mann (Anchor) — dissents, calling the climax-decline-climax structural reading unconvincing
f
Asaph The earliest recoverable Greek text reads Asaph, the Levitical psalmist, where the genealogy requires Asa, king of Judah. The two names come from different Hebrew roots and are not variant spellings of the same word. Manuscript evidence favors Asaph strongly enough that most textual critics treat it as the original reading.↑
Sources
  • Metzger (Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament) — rates the Asaph reading "almost certain"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — reads it as a deliberate allusion to the psalmist, not a scribal error
  • Carson (EBC) — rejects the deliberate-allusion theory, citing looser ancient naming conventions
  • Keener (A Commentary on the Gospel of Matthew) — treats the allusion as a known Jewish exegetical technique, not an idiosyncratic move
g
Amōs The earliest recoverable Greek text reads Amos, the eighth-century prophet, where the genealogy requires Amon, king of Judah and father of Josiah. Unlike the parallel case at Matthew 1:7, Amos and Amon come from unrelated Hebrew names rather than variant spellings of one word. Manuscript evidence favors Amos strongly enough that most textual critics treat it as the original reading.↑
Sources
  • Metzger (Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament) — rates the Amos reading "almost certain," calling the evidence "nearly the same" as the parallel Asaph case
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — less confident this reflects deliberate allusion than the parallel Asaph case, since "Amos" already circulates as a plain LXX spelling confusion for Amon
  • France (NICNT) — suggests manuscripts of Chronicles reading "Amos" for the king reflect the Gospel's own later influence on copyists
  • Turner (BECNT) — treats the same manuscript evidence as a source the genealogy inherited, not a consequence of it
h
Amōs The same name, Amos the prophet rather than Amon the king, recurs here in the verse's own repeated "fathered" formula. The manuscript evidence is identical to the first occurrence.↑
i
Iechonian Matthew's clause moves directly from Josiah to Jeconiah, omitting Jehoiakim, the king named as the intervening generation in 1 Chronicles 3:15-16. Greek manuscripts of the Old Testament sometimes spell Jehoiakim and Jehoiachin (Jeconiah) identically, a documented confusion that may account for the compression. Whether the missing generation is an inherited textual accident or a deliberate compositional device remains disputed.↑
Usually jechonias, jeconiah
Sources
  • Willker (A Textual Commentary on the Greek Gospels) — rates the shorter reading, without an added "Jehoiakim" clause, as clearly original
  • Turner (BECNT) — judges the manuscript insertion of Jehoiakim a later harmonization with 1 Chronicles, producing an extra, fifteenth generation
  • Albright & Mann (Anchor) — reads "Jechoniah" here as standing for Jehoiakim himself
  • Hagner (WBC) — derives the missing generation from 1 Chronicles 3:15's naming of Jehoiakim's own brothers

adelphous Only one brother of Jeconiah is named anywhere in the Old Testament record. Commentators disagree on who "his brothers" denotes: Jeconiah's own royal siblings, the wider company of Jews deported with him, or brothers belonging instead to Jehoiakim, the king Matthew's text skips.↑
Usually brother
Sources
  • Gundry (Matthew) — reads "his brothers" as the fellow Jewish exiles deported with Jeconiah
  • Brown (The Birth of the Messiah) — reads the phrase as carried over from Jehoiakim's own brothers, named at 1 Chronicles 3:15
  • Blomberg (NAC) — reads the brothers as standing for the nation of Israel at the deportation
  • Boring (The New Interpreter's Bible) — reads the plural as a deliberate theological gesture rather than a claim about specific people

metoikesias The Greek word denotes a change of residence and served in Jewish Greek usage as the standard term for the Babylonian deportation. The Septuagint applies the same word to the 597 BC deportation that took Jeconiah himself captive, a different event from the destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC under Zedekiah.↑
Usually carrying away
Sources
  • Levine & Brettler (The Jewish Annotated New Testament) — dates the deportation to 597 BCE, a decade before the Temple's destruction
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — reads the phrase as marking a structural hinge in the genealogy rather than a precise date
  • Gundry (Matthew) — connects the term to the Septuagint's account of the 597 BC deportation in 2 Kings 24:10-16
l
Salathiēl The Hebrew text of 1 Chronicles 3:19 names Zerubbabel as the son of Pedaiah, not Shealtiel (Salathiel). Every other biblical reference to Zerubbabel, including the Septuagint's own rendering of that same verse, calls him son of Shealtiel. Proposed explanations include a levirate marriage, a shift in family headship after Shealtiel died without an heir, and a substitution driven by the curse pronounced on Jeconiah's own offspring in Jeremiah 22.↑
Usually shealtiel
Sources
  • Gundry (Matthew) — proposes that the curse on Jeconiah's immediate offspring drove a substitution of Shealtiel for the disqualified Pedaiah
  • Turner (BECNT) — suggests a levirate marriage, with Pedaiah raising an heir for a childless Shealtiel
  • Albright & Mann (Anchor) — reads the discrepancy as a confusion already present in the Greek textual tradition, with Zerubbabel's identity merged with a cousin's
  • Gill (Exposition of the Old and New Testament) — reports an alternate reading, attributed to the medieval exegete Kimchi, in which Zerubbabel counts as Shealtiel's grandson rather than his son

egennēthē Every other link in the genealogy uses the active verb egennēsen, "fathered." Here the verb shifts to the passive egennēthē, "was born," with Jesus as its subject and Mary marked as the source. Joseph never appears as the grammatical subject of a begetting verb applied to Jesus.↑

  • Orthodox Study Bible — states the pronoun is feminine in Greek, referring only to Mary

mnēsteutheisēs First-century Jewish betrothal was a legally binding first stage of marriage, distinct from marriage only in that the couple had not yet begun living together. A betrothed woman could be divorced or widowed, and infidelity during betrothal counted as adultery. No single English word for "betrothed" or "engaged" carries the same legal weight today.↑
Usually espoused, betrothed
Sources
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — betrothal created "a legal state of marriage, with attendant possibilities of adultery, divorce, and widowhood"
  • France (NICNT) — Joseph's dilemma was never whether to divorce Mary but only whether to do so publicly or privately
  • Brown (Birth of the Messiah) — supplies fuller rabbinic sourcing (Mishnah Ketubbot, Kethuboth) for the same two-stage structure
  • JANT — corroborates the rabbinic sourcing while noting the rabbinic ideal may not reflect first-century practice
  • Keener (A Commentary on the Gospel of Matthew) — reads continued restraint after betrothal as culturally notable rather than a neutral legal default

pneumatos Pneuma spans a range from ordinary wind or breath to the animating principle in a person, an incorporeal spirit or demon, and the divine Spirit. Greek grammar does not resolve which sense is meant in a given case. The presence or absence of the article is sometimes read as marking the divine Spirit specifically, though this pattern is not applied consistently and remains disputed.↑
Usually spirit, ghost, wind
Sources
  • Nolland (NIGTC; also JSNT 62 [1996]) — distinguishes the LXX idiom for conception, which can mark a male role, from the idiom used here, which does not
  • Davies & Allison (ICC) — "the word is definite from the nature of the case" despite the missing article
  • Brown (Birth of the Messiah) — the Spirit is never presented as the male element in the conception, being neither male nor a sexual partner
  • Lagrand (NovT 22 [1980]) — reads the grammar differently, assigning Mary the "male" role and the Spirit the "female" role

dikaios δικαιοσύνη, δίκαιος, and δικαιόω range in sense from personal moral righteousness to covenantal or forensic justice. Translators and commentators divide over which sense a given occurrence carries. No single English word covers that full range.↑
Usually righteous, right, righteous one
Sources
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary, G1342 (δίκαιος) / G1343 (δικαιοσύνη) — glosses δίκαιος "equitable (in character or act)," δικαιοσύνη "equity... specially (Christian) justification," spanning both senses
  • Abbott-Smith (Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — two-tier entry: broad sense "conformity to the Divine will in purpose, thought, and action," narrower sense "justice"
  • Benno Przybylski, Righteousness in Matthew and His World of Thought (SNTSMS 41, Cambridge University Press, 1980) — argues Matthew's usage is uniformly ethical, "proper conduct before God," against a Pauline forensic-gift reading
  • Donald A. Hagner — opposing view: some occurrences (5:6, 6:33) denote eschatological "justice" or gift, not ethical performance, explicitly "pace Przybylski"
  • W.D. Davies & Dale C. Allison (ICC, Matthew 1-7) — favors the "conduct" reading at 5:10, listing allied scholars (Barth, Fenton, Gundry, Luz, Przybylski, Strecker, Reumann, Ziesler) against dissenter Giesen

kai Grammarians disagree whether this conjunction is adversative ("though righteous, yet unwilling") or continuative ("being righteous, and so unwilling"). A widely cited grammar is invoked for the adversative reading, but its actual text classifies the clause as causal, not concessive.↑
Usually both, neither, likewise
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — reads kai as a mild adversative, citing BDF §417
  • Blass, Debrunner & Funk, A Greek Grammar of the New Testament (1961), §418(1) — names Matthew 1:19 as its own example of a causal, not concessive, participle
  • Wallace, Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics (1996) — confirms the general concessive/causal participle distinction without ruling on this verse

deigmatisai Manuscripts split between this reading, "to expose publicly," and the stronger compound paradeigmatisai, "to hold up as a public example." Modern critical editions favor the shorter reading; the Byzantine tradition and Textus Receptus favor the compound. Nearly every translation renders the verse the same way regardless of which is followed.↑

kuriou κύριος ranges from an ordinary term of respectful address ("sir," "master") through a disciple's title for a living teacher to the full divine name. In the Septuagint it is the standard reverential substitute for the unpronounced Hebrew Tetragrammaton, YHWH. When the New Testament quotes an Old Testament passage naming YHWH, the Greek text carries the same word, κύριος, with no orthographic marker distinguishing it from the word's ordinary human sense.↑
Usually lord, master, sir
Sources
  • Abbott-Smith (Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — states κύριος is used "in LXX (subst.) chiefly for יהוה, also for בַּעַל, אָדוֹן," cataloguing the full range from genitive-of-possession ("master of the house") through vocative address ("sir") to the full divine title
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary, G2962 — "supreme in authority, i.e. controller; by implication, Master (as a respectful title)"
  • John Nolland (NIGTC) — cautions against over-reading an anarthrous κύριος as inherently a divine title, noting Psalm 110:1's "my lord" is ordinary court address to a king
  • R.T. France (NICNT) — on Matthew's citation of Isaiah 40:3, applying to Jesus a text whose κύριος names God himself constitutes "a remarkable christological claim"
  • Martin Hengel, "Sit at My Right Hand!" in Studies in Early Christology (T&T Clark, 1995) — traces how Psalm 110:1's double κύριος became the primary vehicle for the earliest church's confession of Jesus's exalted status

paralabein παραλαμβάνω is an ordinary verb for taking a person along or to oneself, used elsewhere in Matthew for taking children on a journey, taking companions up a mountain, and marching a prisoner into custody. Applied to a wife, it marks the second stage of Jewish marriage law, bringing a betrothed woman into the groom's household. No lexicon entry treats the marital use as a distinct sense apart from the verb's other objects.↑
Usually received, taken, took
Sources
  • Abbott-Smith (Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — groups Matthew 1:20's "take as wife" in the same sense as taking companions up a mountain or a prisoner into custody
  • Davies & Allison (ICC) — cites Herodotus 4.155, two passages of Josephus, and the rabbinic term for bringing a bride into the groom's house
  • Albright & Mann (Anchor) — describes betrothal and the taking of the bride into the groom's house as the two legal stages of Jewish marriage
  • France (NICNT) — reads the verb as carrying a sense of welcome, not mere dutiful compliance

The article τό substantizes the participle into an unspecified subject, an ordinary construction that recurs in Matthew's own fulfillment formulas. The neuter form leaves the child's gender undisclosed until the following verse.↑
Usually begat, born, begotten

pneumatos Pneuma spans a range from ordinary wind or breath to the animating principle in a person, an incorporeal spirit or demon, and the divine Spirit. Greek grammar does not resolve which sense is meant in a given case. The presence or absence of the article is sometimes read as marking the divine Spirit specifically, though this pattern is not applied consistently and remains disputed.↑
Usually spirit, ghost, wind
Sources
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary, G4151 — base sense "a current of air, i.e. breath (blast) or a breeze," extending by analogy to human soul, angel, demon, or God's own Spirit
  • Abbott-Smith (Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — five-sense entry (wind/breath, vital principle, disposition, incorporeal being, Holy Spirit); article's presence "as a rule" marks the Spirit as Person/Divine Power versus an anarthrous "operation, influence, or gift"
  • John Nolland (NIGTC) and W.D. Davies & Dale C. Allison (ICC) — both argue, independently, that "ἐκ πνεύματος ἁγίου" denotes divine creative power rather than a paternal/male generative role, Nolland via LXX idiom, Davies & Allison via grammatical gender (Greek πνεῦμα is neuter, Hebrew רוּחַ is feminine)
  • SBLGNT text itself — πνεῦμα ἅγιον is anarthrous at Matthew 1:18, 1:20, and 3:11 but articular at 12:32 and 28:19, a distributional pattern whose significance remains disputed absent a dedicated grammar treatment

laon λαός in Biblical Greek usage denotes the covenantal people of Israel, standing opposite τὰ ἔθνη, the nations. Matthew glosses the same phrase with the appositive "my people, Israel" two chapters later, at 2:6. Whether the referent here stays ethnic Israel alone or already anticipates a wider Jewish and Gentile people remains a live, unresolved question.↑
Usually people
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — reads "his people" as a remnant within Israel, not yet inclusive of Gentiles
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — takes the referent as ethnic Israel at this point in the narrative, later both narrowed and widened
  • Brown (The Birth of the Messiah) — states plainly that "his people" already includes both Jews and Gentiles for Matthew
  • Hagner (WBC) — ties an inclusive reading to Matthew 1:1's "son of David" and "son of Abraham" titles, anchored to the ekklēsia of 16:18
  • Boring (New Interpreter's Bible) — declines to specify the referent, locating the eventual dispute over "his people" at 21:43 and 27:25

hamartiōn The root sense is a missing of the mark, the failure of a thrown spear or arrow to hit its target. Classical usage extends this to any fault, failure, or error of judgment, then narrows in philosophical and religious writing to guilt or moral sin. The New Testament and the Septuagint before it use only the narrowed sense.↑
Usually sin
Sources
  • Davies & Allison (ICC) — propose the ἀνομία/ἁμαρτία shift responds to the σώσει/Ἰησοῦν pun and to ἁμαρτία's use in Isaiah 53 and early confessional formulas
  • Turner (BECNT) — identifies Psalm 130:8 as the closest Old Testament verbal parallel
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — calls the psalm "verbally closest to Mt. 1:21" while noting the wording still differs
  • Hagner (WBC) — proposes the clause may be a targumic rendering of the psalm

holon τοῦτο δὲ ὅλον, "all this," introduces the citation before the events it explains are fully narrated. Commentators disagree on how far the phrase reaches. Some read it as spanning forward through the child's naming, not yet narrated at this point. Others read it as bounded, covering only the pregnancy and the angel's appearance already recounted.↑
Usually all, whole, entirely
Sources
  • France (NICNT) — reads "all this" as spanning forward through the naming of the child
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — reads the phrase as bounded, covering only Mary's pregnancy and the angel's appearance
  • Kupp (Matthew's Emmanuel) — ties the phrase's fulfillment to the meaning of the name Emmanuel, not the bare historical events alone
  • Chrysostom (Homilies on Matthew, cited in Mitch & Sri, CCSS) — treats Joseph's own act of naming the child as part of what is being accomplished

kuriou The verse names God as the prophecy's ultimate source, ὑπὸ κυρίου, and the prophet as its instrumental channel, διὰ τοῦ προφήτου. Matthew uses this fuller two-part form only once elsewhere, at 2:15. Every other fulfillment-formula citation in the Gospel names only the channel. One proposal reads the ultimate-source phrase as a signal that the "son" named in the coming quotation is God's own son. Commentators are divided: most reject this reading, one accepts it, and one reads the phrase instead as an echo of "the angel of the Lord" two verses earlier.↑
Usually lord, master, sir
Sources
  • Pesch (Biblica 48 [1967]) — proposes that "by the Lord" signals a Son-of-God reading of the quotation's "son," cited independently by France and Nolland
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — rejects Pesch's proposal, reading "by the Lord" instead as an echo of "the angel of the Lord" at 1:20
  • France (NICNT) — independently rejects Pesch's proposal, noting Matthew nowhere else links Jesus' Spirit-conception directly to a "Son of God" title
  • Davies & Allison (ICC) — accepts Pesch's proposal as "the more plausible" of two explanations on offer
  • Abbott-Smith (Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — confirms ὑπό and διά together mark a real, if inconsistently applied, distinction between ultimate agent and instrumental channel, citing Matthew 1:22 and 2:15

plērōthēi This is the first of ten fulfillment-formula citations in Matthew's Gospel. Commentators divide over what kind of fulfillment the verb claims. Some read a strict, one-to-one predictive fulfillment. Others read a looser, typological pattern, in which an earlier word or event takes on a fuller meaning only later.↑
Usually fulfilled, filled, full
Sources
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — classifies this citation as claiming the eschatological fulfillment of a prophecy already given a partial, inadequate historical fulfillment
  • Turner (BECNT) — calls a strictly predictive reading of Matthew's fulfillment formula "simplistic at best"
  • Albright & Mann (Anchor) — root Matthew's method in Qumran pesher exegesis, citing Brownlee's study of Dead Sea Scrolls interpretive principles
  • Hagner (WBC) — describes Matthew's compositional method here as "his midrashic haggadah"

parthenos The underlying Hebrew, almah, denotes a young woman of childbearing age. It does not specify virginity the way the more exact Hebrew word bethulah does. The Septuagint renders almah with parthenos at only one other place among the word's seven occurrences in the Hebrew Bible, using the more common neanis, "young woman," elsewhere. Commentators disagree on how much weight this earlier translation choice can bear, since the Septuagint's rendering of Isaiah is independently documented as a loose translation.↑
Usually virgin
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — citing Seeligmann's study of the Septuagint's translation technique in Isaiah, judges the Greek word choice unable to bear much interpretive weight on its own
  • France (NICNT) — reads the Septuagint's choice as tracing back to something unusual in Isaiah's own Hebrew wording
  • Davies & Allison (ICC) — confirms neanis, not parthenos, as the Septuagint's ordinary rendering of almah
  • Brown (Birth of the Messiah) — reads the Septuagint's choice as deliberate but not necessarily a signal of virginity preserved through birth
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — locates the "virgin" reading in an unintended combination of two separate, otherwise ordinary translation choices

kalesousin The Septuagint reads "you will call," second person singular, addressed to an unspecified hearer. Matthew's quotation reads "they will call," third person plural, with no stated subject. Commentators disagree over the shift's origin. Some read it as a free rendering. Others argue the plural was already present in the Hebrew or Greek text being quoted.↑
Usually called, call, invited
Sources
  • Menken (NovT 43 [2001]) — argues the plural was already present in a revised Septuagint text, tracing to a Hebrew variant close to the reading in the Isaiah scroll from Qumran
  • France (NICNT) — reads the plural as Matthew's own free composition, with no indication it came from any other source
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — surveys several explanations, preferring one that gives Joseph, not only Mary, a role in the naming
  • Brown (Birth of the Messiah) — adds a Dead Sea Scrolls reading, qr', as a fourth witness alongside the Masoretic Text, the Septuagint, and Matthew's own text

theos Commentators divide sharply over how much theological weight the closing clause carries. Some read a direct claim that Jesus is God. Others read a narrower statement of divine presence and favor, left to develop across the rest of the Gospel. The Greek word order, "with us God" rather than "God with us," figures in the disagreement: several commentators who resist the stronger reading argue the phrase functions adverbially, "with us is God," rather than as a predication identifying Jesus as God outright.↑
Usually god
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — reads the clause in the fullest sense, that Jesus as God's Son is also God himself among his people
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — gives a five-point argument against reading the clause as an ontological "Jesus is God" claim
  • Davies & Allison (ICC) — reads the phrase adverbially, "with us is God," denying a full incarnational intent while affirming a unique divine presence
  • Carson (EBC) — argues for the strong reading from Isaiah's own theology, linking the name to Isaiah 9:6's "Mighty God"
  • Kupp (Matthew's Emmanuel) — reframes the question as functional rather than ontological, calling Jesus the agent of God's presence rather than adjudicating a "Son of God" claim directly

kuriou κύριος ranges from an ordinary term of respectful address ("sir," "master") through a disciple's title for a living teacher to the full divine name. In the Septuagint it is the standard reverential substitute for the unpronounced Hebrew Tetragrammaton, YHWH. When the New Testament quotes an Old Testament passage naming YHWH, the Greek text carries the same word, κύριος, with no orthographic marker distinguishing it from the word's ordinary human sense.↑
Usually lord, master, sir
Sources
  • Abbott-Smith (Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — states κύριος is used "in LXX (subst.) chiefly for יהוה, also for בַּעַל, אָדוֹן," cataloguing the full range from genitive-of-possession ("master of the house") through vocative address ("sir") to the full divine title
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary, G2962 — "supreme in authority, i.e. controller; by implication, Master (as a respectful title)"
  • John Nolland (NIGTC) — cautions against over-reading an anarthrous κύριος as inherently a divine title, noting Psalm 110:1's "my lord" is ordinary court address to a king
  • R.T. France (NICNT) — on Matthew's citation of Isaiah 40:3, applying to Jesus a text whose κύριος names God himself constitutes "a remarkable christological claim"
  • Martin Hengel, "Sit at My Right Hand!" in Studies in Early Christology (T&T Clark, 1995) — traces how Psalm 110:1's double κύριος became the primary vehicle for the earliest church's confession of Jesus's exalted status

eginōsken The verb carries a well-attested sense, "to have relations with," used the same way for the same verb three times in Genesis 4. The imperfect tense describes the state lasting up to the birth. A minority reading takes the imperfect's own aspect as suggesting relations began only afterward, a reading directly rejected elsewhere in the commentary literature.↑
Usually know, known, knew
Sources
  • Abbott-Smith and LSJ — both name Matthew 1:25 as a citation instance of this euphemistic sense, tracing it through classical usage as well as the Septuagint
  • Turner (BECNT) — reports a minority "inceptive imperfect" reading, citing W. Allen (1912), under which the tense itself implies relations began after the birth
  • Carson (EBC) — rejects an imperfect-tense argument for continued celibacy after the birth, naming and answering John McHugh's version of it directly

heōs hou "Until" marks the point up to which Joseph and Mary had no relations. Whether the construction says anything about what followed is disputed. Readings range from a plain "no implication either way," through a translation choice meant to sidestep the question, to a claim that ordinary relations resumed once the stated point was reached.↑
Sources
  • Mitch & Sri (CCSS) — read the grammar as settling nothing either way, leaving Catholic doctrine on the question grounded elsewhere
  • France (NICNT) — reads "until" as most naturally implying resumption of ordinary marital relations after the birth
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — translates the word "before" rather than "until" specifically to avoid implying anything about what came after
  • Wright, Biblical and Ancient Greek Linguistics 13 (2025) — argues on formal-semantic grounds that the construction carries an obligatory inference of resumption, against the wider scholarly consensus
