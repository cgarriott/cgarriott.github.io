Notes

a
geneseōs Βίβλος γενέσεως repeats a rare phrase from the Greek Septuagint's own Genesis 2:4 and 5:1, both introducing accounts of a beginning. Commentators disagree on how much weight that echo carries, from a light stylistic flourish to a claim that the Gospel opens as a new act of creation. They also disagree on how far the resulting title reaches, from the seventeen-verse genealogy alone to the entire Gospel.↑
Usually birth, natural
Sources
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — reads the phrase as a deliberate "new Genesis," extending the title to the whole Gospel
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — reads the echo as real but rhetorically modest, and confines the title to the genealogy alone
  • Hagner (WBC) — treats the echo as carrying near-maximal theological weight while still limiting the title's reach to the genealogy
  • Boring (The New Interpreter's Bible) — argues from the Septuagint's own frequent use of "book" that the title covers the whole Gospel
  • Goulder (Midrash and Lection in Matthew) — reads the echo as a lectionary marker, tying the Gospel's opening to Genesis's place in the synagogue reading cycle
b
christou This occurrence opens a chiasm with the same word at 1:17, bounding the genealogy between two uses of the title. Most readings treat every occurrence in the surrounding chapters as titular, but one holds that this first occurrence, alone among roughly eighteen uses in the Gospel, already carries more than titular force. Χριστός is ambiguous between a title, the Anointed One or Messiah, and a fixed second name for Jesus. Scholars disagree on where the word tips from title into name, and some read it as doing both at once. The verb χρίω behind it means to anoint, so every anointing in the New Testament is the same word as the name. Χριστιανός is the partisan noun built on it, formed with a Latin suffix meaning someone's people, as the Herodians were Herod's. It occurs three times, and what it meant at Antioch is disputed.↑
Usually christ, jesus
Sources
  • Carson (EBC) — names 1:1 itself, alongside 1:16 and 1:18, as one of the rare places where "Christ" is not purely titular
  • Hagner (WBC) — the word's proximity to "son of David" in the same verse keeps titular meaning close at hand even where the term functions as a name

adelphous Isaac's brother Ishmael and Jacob's brother Esau go unnamed at the parallel points earlier in the same sentence. Commentators read the asymmetry as a deliberate signal, most often tracing it to the twelve tribes of Israel through Judah's reception of the royal scepter in Genesis 49:10.↑
Usually brother
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — reads the phrase as "an allusion to the twelve tribes who form the locus of the people of God and the pattern for the twelve apostles"
  • Gundry (Matthew: A Commentary on His Literary and Theological Art) — traces it instead to an interest in the people of God as a brotherhood, where "Judah and his brothers prefigure the brotherhood of the church"
  • Hagner (WBC) — reads it as pointing to the twelve tribes of Israel, whose theological fullness comes to bear on Jesus as their culmination
  • Hood (The Messiah, His Brothers, and the Nations) — reads it as foreshadowing Jesus's own self-sacrifice on behalf of his brothers

Aram Matthew's Greek reads Ἀράμ, the same spelling as the Septuagint's text of 1 Chronicles 2:9–10. The Septuagint itself is inconsistent: the same figure is spelled Ἀρραν at Ruth 4:19, and both spellings differ from the Hebrew form behind them, Ram. Translations split on which name to print: some transliterate Matthew's own wording as "Aram," others harmonize to the familiar Hebrew "Ram."↑
Usually ram
Sources
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — glosses Ἀράμ as "of Hebrew origin; Aram (i.e. Ram)"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — traces the double spelling to an internal doublet in the Septuagint's own text of 1 Chronicles 2:9–10
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — independently confirm the same Septuagint-internal mechanism from the primary texts
  • Wasserman (Evangelical Textual Criticism) — notes that confidence in the parallel reading at Luke 3:33 has since weakened in a later critical edition

basilea David alone among the genealogy's named figures carries a title. Scholars read this as marking a structural pivot in the list, closing its first section before the pattern shifts toward decline and a later climax. A minority view treats the title mainly as standard Old Testament phrasing for David rather than a deliberate structural marker.↑
Usually king
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — reads the title as a deliberate addition beyond Matthew's source text, anticipating Jesus's own royal title
  • Hagner (WBC) — reads the addition as strengthening the link between David and Jesus as "the Davidic, messianic king, an important motif in Matthew"
  • Gundry (Matthew: A Commentary on His Literary and Theological Art) — reads Matthew's adoption of the Old Testament designation as putting David forward as "a prototype of Jesus" and closing the genealogy's first section
  • Albright and Mann (Anchor) — dissents, calling the climax-decline-climax structural reading unconvincing
f
Asaph The earliest recoverable Greek text reads Asaph, the Levitical psalmist, where the genealogy requires Asa, king of Judah. The two names come from different Hebrew roots and are not variant spellings of the same word. Manuscript evidence favors Asaph strongly enough that most textual critics treat it as the original reading.↑
Sources
  • Metzger (A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament) — rates the Asaph reading "almost certain"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — calls it "a deliberate ploy to evoke yet more of the history of the people of God"
  • Carson (EBC) — rejects the deliberate-allusion theory as "too cryptic to be believable," pointing to looser ancient orthography
  • Keener (A Commentary on the Gospel of Matthew) — treats the allusion as a known Jewish exegetical technique, not an idiosyncratic move
g
Amōs The earliest recoverable Greek text reads Amos, the eighth-century prophet, where the genealogy requires Amon, king of Judah and father of Josiah. Unlike the parallel case at Matthew 1:7, Amos and Amon come from unrelated Hebrew names rather than variant spellings of one word. Manuscript evidence favors Amos strongly enough that most textual critics treat it as the original reading.↑
Sources
  • Metzger (A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament) — rates the Amos reading "almost certain," calling the evidence "nearly the same" as the parallel Asaph case
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — finds a deliberate allusion "much less certain" here than at the parallel Asaph case, since Amos is "well attested as an LXX reading for Amon"
  • France (NICNT) — reads the manuscripts of Chronicles that name the king Amos as "likely to be due to the influence of Matthew"
  • Turner (BECNT) — treats the same manuscript evidence as a source the genealogy inherited, not a consequence of it

Amōs The same name, Amos the prophet rather than Amon the king, recurs here in the verse's own repeated "fathered" formula. The manuscript evidence is identical to the first occurrence.↑
i
Iechonian Matthew's clause moves directly from Josiah to Jeconiah, omitting Jehoiakim, the king named as the intervening generation in 1 Chronicles 3:15-16. Greek manuscripts of the Old Testament sometimes spell Jehoiakim and Jehoiachin (Jeconiah) identically, a documented confusion that may account for the compression. Whether the missing generation is an inherited textual accident or a deliberate compositional device remains disputed.↑
Usually jeconiah, jechonias
Sources
  • Willker (A Textual Commentary on the Greek Gospels) — rates the shorter reading, without an added "Jehoiakim" clause, as clearly original
  • Turner (BECNT) — judges the insertion of Jehoiakim a later variant "motivated by the desire to conform Matthew's genealogy" to 1 Chronicles, producing a fifteenth generation
  • Albright and Mann (Anchor) — presume that "Jechoniah (1 Chron 3:16) is here an alternative for king Jehoiakim"
  • Hagner (WBC) — derives the missing generation from 1 Chronicles 3:15's naming of Jehoiakim's own brothers

adelphous Only one brother of Jeconiah is named anywhere in the Old Testament record. Commentators disagree on who "his brothers" denotes: Jeconiah's own royal siblings, the wider company of Jews deported with him, or brothers belonging instead to Jehoiakim, the king Matthew's text skips.↑

metoikesias The Greek word denotes a change of residence and served in Jewish Greek usage as the standard term for the Babylonian deportation. The Septuagint applies the same word to the 597 BC deportation that took Jeconiah himself captive, a different event from the destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC under Zedekiah.↑
Usually carrying away
Sources
  • Levine and Brettler (The Jewish Annotated New Testament) — dates the deportation to 597 BCE, a decade before the Temple's destruction
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — takes the phrase to mean "in the period in which the deportation to Babylon took place" rather than a date within a reign
  • Gundry (Matthew: A Commentary on His Literary and Theological Art) — connects the term to the Septuagint's account of the 597 BC deportation in 2 Kings 24:10-16

Salathiēl The Hebrew text of 1 Chronicles 3:19 names Zerubbabel as the son of Pedaiah, not Shealtiel (Salathiel). Every other biblical reference to Zerubbabel, including the Septuagint's own rendering of that same verse, calls him son of Shealtiel. Proposed explanations include a levirate marriage, a shift in family headship after Shealtiel died without an heir, and a substitution driven by the curse pronounced on Jeconiah's own offspring in Jeremiah 22.↑
Usually shealtiel
Sources
  • Gundry (Matthew: A Commentary on His Literary and Theological Art) — proposes that the curse on Jeconiah's line led to "replacement of his son Pedaiah with Salathiel, who came from another branch of David's family"
  • Turner (BECNT) — suggests Pedaiah "entered a levirate marriage with Shealtiel's widow to raise up children for his brother"
  • Albright and Mann (Anchor) — reads the discrepancy as a confusion already present in the Greek textual tradition, with Zerubbabel's identity merged with a cousin's
  • Gill (Exposition of the Old and New Testament) — reports an alternate reading, attributed to the medieval exegete Kimchi, in which Zerubbabel counts as Shealtiel's grandson rather than his son

p
genesis Two disputes attach to this single word. Textually, the Textus Receptus and the Byzantine tradition read "gennēsis" ("birth") where every other collated edition reads "genesis" ("origin, becoming"), a substitution Metzger attributes to scribes narrowing an unfamiliar broader sense toward a more specialized one. Separately, translators render the agreed-upon "genesis" here as "birth," obscuring its repetition of the same word opening the genealogy at 1:1. Commentators disagree on how much is lost: some read "birth" as a defensible narrowing that still preserves the link, others as a translation that erases a deliberate authorial echo.↑
Usually birth, natural
Sources
  • Metzger (A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament) — rates "genesis" {B}, attributes the variant to scribal narrowing toward a more familiar word
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — reads "birth" as a narrowing translation that still takes up 1:1's usage
  • France (NICNT) — argues "birth" loses a deliberate verbal link to 1:1 that only repeating "genesis" would preserve
  • Turner (BECNT) — uses the repetition as evidence against reading 1:1's "genesis" narrowly as "genealogy"
  • Brown (The Birth of the Messiah) — reports a minority view (Davies) that the direction of corruption may run the other way, then sets it aside

mnēsteutheisēs First-century Jewish betrothal was a legally binding first stage of marriage, distinct from marriage only in that the couple had not yet begun living together. A betrothed woman could be divorced or widowed, and infidelity during betrothal counted as adultery. No single English word for "betrothed" or "engaged" carries the same legal weight today.↑
Usually espoused, betrothed
Sources
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — betrothal created "a legal state of marriage, with attendant possibilities of adultery, divorce, and widowhood"
  • France (NICNT) — Joseph's dilemma was never whether to divorce Mary but only whether to do so publicly or privately
  • Brown (The Birth of the Messiah) — supplies fuller rabbinic sourcing (Mishnah Ketubbot, Kethuboth) for the same two-stage structure
  • The Jewish Annotated New Testament — corroborates the rabbinic sourcing while noting the rabbinic ideal may not reflect first-century practice
  • Keener (A Commentary on the Gospel of Matthew) — reads continued restraint after betrothal as culturally notable rather than a neutral legal default

pneumatos The phrase is grammatically anarthrous, lacking a definite article, a pattern that recurs at 1:20 and 3:11 but not at 12:32 or 28:19. Some translations render this "a Holy Spirit" rather than "the Holy Spirit" on that basis. Commentators who address the grammar directly argue the missing article does not indicate an indefinite spirit, since the referent is fixed by context regardless. Pneuma spans a range from ordinary wind or breath to the animating principle in a person, an incorporeal spirit or demon, and the divine Spirit. Greek grammar does not resolve which sense is meant in a given case. The presence or absence of the article is sometimes read as marking the divine Spirit specifically, though this pattern is not applied consistently and remains disputed.↑
Usually spirit, ghost, wind
Sources
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — distinguishes the LXX idiom for conception, which can mark a male role, from the idiom used here, which does not
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — "the word is definite from the nature of the case" despite the missing article
  • Brown (The Birth of the Messiah) — the Spirit is never presented as the male element in the conception, being neither male nor a sexual partner
  • Lagrand ("How Was the Virgin Mary 'Like a Man'?," NovT) — reads the grammar differently, assigning Mary the "male" role and the Spirit the "female" role

dikaios Two readings divide the commentaries. One takes Joseph's righteousness as strict law-observance, standing in tension with his wish to spare Mary public disgrace. The other takes his righteousness as already including that mercy, with no tension to resolve. Commentators split close to evenly on which reading is correct. δικαιοσύνη, δίκαιος, and δικαιόω range in sense from personal moral righteousness to covenantal or forensic justice. Translators and commentators divide over which sense a given occurrence carries. No single English word covers that full range.↑
Usually righteous, right, righteous one
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) and France (NICNT) — read dikaios as law-abiding, with the following clause set against it
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — rejects any tension, reading dikaios as explaining the divorce decision itself
  • Brown (The Birth of the Messiah) — sides with Nolland, calling this the only convincing account among three
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — sides with Turner and France, and reports Ulrich Luz taking the opposite view

kai Grammarians disagree whether this conjunction is adversative ("though righteous, yet unwilling") or continuative ("being righteous, and so unwilling"). A widely cited grammar is invoked for the adversative reading, but its actual text classifies the clause as causal, not concessive.↑
Usually both, neither, indeed
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — reads kai as a mild adversative, citing Blass, Debrunner and Funk
  • Blass, Debrunner and Funk (A Greek Grammar of the New Testament) — names Matthew 1:19 as its own example of a causal, not concessive, participle
  • Wallace (Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics) — confirms the general concessive/causal participle distinction without ruling on this verse

deigmatisai Manuscripts split between this reading, "to expose publicly," and the stronger compound paradeigmatisai, "to hold up as a public example." Modern critical editions favor the shorter reading, the Byzantine tradition and Textus Receptus the compound. Nearly every translation renders the verse the same way regardless of which is followed.↑
Sources
  • Translators Amalgamated Greek New Testament — NA28, NA27, Tyndale House, SBL, WH, and Tregelles all read deigmatisai, against the Byzantine and Textus Receptus reading paradeigmatisai

  • Nolland (NIGTC) — reads the word as referring to a formal public trial, not merely ordinary social visibility
  • Metzger (A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament) — contains no entry on this variant, a checked absence

kuriou κύριος ranges from an ordinary term of respectful address ("sir," "master") through a disciple's title for a living teacher to the full divine name. In the Septuagint it is the standard reverential substitute for the unpronounced Hebrew Tetragrammaton. When the New Testament quotes an Old Testament passage that names it, the Greek text carries the same word, κύριος, with no orthographic marker distinguishing it from the word's ordinary human sense.↑
Usually lord, master, sir
Sources
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — states κύριος is used "in LXX (subst.) chiefly for [the divine name], also for בַּעַל, אָדוֹן," cataloguing the full range from genitive-of-possession ("master of the house") through vocative address ("sir") to the full divine title
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — "supreme in authority, i.e. controller; by implication, Master (as a respectful title)"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — cautions against over-reading an anarthrous κύριος as inherently a divine title, noting Psalm 110:1's "my lord" is ordinary court address to a king
  • France (NICNT) — on Matthew's citation of Isaiah 40:3, applying to Jesus a text whose κύριος names God himself constitutes "a remarkable christological claim"
  • Hengel (Studies in Early Christology) — traces how Psalm 110:1's double κύριος became the primary vehicle for the earliest church's confession of Jesus's exalted status

paralabein παραλαμβάνω is an ordinary verb for taking a person along or to oneself, used elsewhere in Matthew for taking children on a journey, taking companions up a mountain, and marching a prisoner into custody. Applied to a wife, it marks the second stage of Jewish marriage law, bringing a betrothed woman into the groom's household. No lexicon entry treats the marital use as a distinct sense apart from the verb's other objects.↑
Usually taken, received, took unto
Sources
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — groups Matthew 1:20's "take as wife" in the same sense as taking companions up a mountain or a prisoner into custody
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — cites Herodotus, two passages of Josephus, and the rabbinic term for bringing a bride into the groom's house
  • Albright and Mann (Anchor) — describes betrothal and the taking of the bride into the groom's house as the two legal stages of Jewish marriage
  • France (NICNT) — reads the verb as carrying a sense of welcome, not mere dutiful compliance

The article τό substantizes the participle into an unspecified subject, an ordinary construction that recurs in Matthew's own fulfillment formulas. The neuter form leaves the child's gender undisclosed until the following verse.↑
Usually begat, born, brought forth
Sources

laon λαός in Biblical Greek usage denotes the covenantal people of Israel, standing opposite τὰ ἔθνη, the nations. Matthew glosses the same phrase with the appositive "my people, Israel" two chapters later, at 2:6. Whether the referent here stays ethnic Israel alone or already anticipates a wider Jewish and Gentile people remains a live, unresolved question.↑
Usually people
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — reads "his people" as a remnant within Israel, not yet inclusive of Gentiles
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — takes the referent as ethnic Israel at this point in the narrative, later both narrowed and widened
  • Brown (The Birth of the Messiah) — states plainly that "his people" already includes both Jews and Gentiles for Matthew

hamartiōn Psalm 130:8 LXX, the closest verbal parallel to this clause, reads "from all his ἀνομίαι," lawless deeds, not ἁμαρτίαι. Proposed reasons for the substitution include an echo pairing σώσει with the name Ἰησοῦν, and ἁμαρτία's currency in Isaiah 53 and early confessional formulas. The parallel to the psalm remains thematic rather than a verbatim quotation. The root sense is a missing of the mark, the failure of a thrown spear or arrow to hit its target. Classical usage extends this to any fault, failure, or error of judgment, then narrows in philosophical and religious writing to guilt or moral sin. The New Testament and the Septuagint before it use only the narrowed sense.↑
Usually sin
Sources
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — propose the ἀνομία/ἁμαρτία shift responds to the σώσει/Ἰησοῦν pun and to ἁμαρτία's use in Isaiah 53 and early confessional formulas
  • Turner (BECNT) — identifies Psalm 130:8 as the closest Old Testament verbal parallel
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — calls the psalm "verbally closest to Mt. 1:21" while noting the wording still differs
  • Hagner (WBC) — proposes the clause may be a targumic rendering of the psalm

kuriou The verse names God as the prophecy's ultimate source, ὑπὸ κυρίου, and the prophet as its instrumental channel, διὰ τοῦ προφήτου. Matthew uses this fuller two-part form only once elsewhere, at 2:15. Every other fulfillment-formula citation in the Gospel names only the channel. One proposal reads the ultimate-source phrase as a signal that the "son" named in the coming quotation is God's own son. Commentators are divided: most reject this reading, one accepts it, and one reads the phrase instead as an echo of "the angel of the Lord" two verses earlier.↑
Usually lord, master, sir
Sources
  • Pesch ("Der Gottessohn im matthäischen Evangelienprolog," Bib) — proposes that "by the Lord" signals a Son-of-God reading of the quotation's "son," cited independently by France and Nolland
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — rejects Pesch's proposal, reading "by the Lord" instead as an echo of "the angel of the Lord" at 1:20
  • France (NICNT) — independently rejects Pesch's proposal, noting Matthew nowhere else links Jesus' Spirit-conception directly to a "Son of God" title
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — accepts Pesch's proposal as "the more plausible" of two explanations on offer
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — confirms ὑπό and διά together mark a real, if inconsistently applied, distinction between ultimate agent and instrumental channel, citing Matthew 1:22 and 2:15

plērōthēi This is the first of ten fulfillment-formula citations in Matthew's Gospel. Commentators divide over what kind of fulfillment the verb claims.   • Nolland (NIGTC) — classifies this citation as claiming the eschatological fulfillment of a prophecy already given a partial, inadequate historical fulfillment

  • Hagner (WBC) — describes Matthew's compositional method here as "his midrashic haggadah"

parthenos The underlying Hebrew, almah, denotes a young woman of childbearing age. It does not specify virginity the way the more exact Hebrew word bethulah does. The Septuagint renders almah with parthenos at only one other place among the word's seven occurrences in the Hebrew Bible, using the more common neanis, "young woman," elsewhere. Commentators disagree on how much weight this earlier translation choice can bear, since the Septuagint's rendering of Isaiah is independently documented as a loose translation.↑
Usually virgin, virginity
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — citing Seeligmann's study of the Septuagint's translation technique in Isaiah, judges the Greek word choice unable to bear much interpretive weight on its own
  • France (NICNT) — reads the Septuagint's choice as tracing back to something unusual in Isaiah's own Hebrew wording
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — confirms neanis, not parthenos, as the Septuagint's ordinary rendering of almah
  • Brown (The Birth of the Messiah) — reads the Septuagint's choice as deliberate but not necessarily a signal of virginity preserved through birth
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — locates the "virgin" reading in an unintended combination of two separate, otherwise ordinary translation choices

kalesousin The Septuagint reads "you will call," second person singular, addressed to an unspecified hearer. Matthew's quotation reads "they will call," third person plural, with no stated subject. Commentators disagree over the shift's origin. Some read it as a free rendering. Others argue the plural was already present in the Hebrew or Greek text being quoted.↑

  • Menken ("The Textual Form of the Quotation from Isaiah 7:14 in Matthew 1:23," NovT) — argues the plural was already present in a revised Septuagint text, tracing to a Hebrew variant close to the reading in the Isaiah scroll from Qumran
  • France (NICNT) — reads the plural as Matthew's own free composition, with no indication it came from any other source

  • Brown (The Birth of the Messiah) — adds a Dead Sea Scrolls reading, qr', as a fourth witness alongside the Masoretic Text, the Septuagint, and Matthew's own text

theos Commentators divide sharply over how much theological weight the closing clause carries. Some read a direct claim that Jesus is God. Others read a narrower statement of divine presence and favor, left to develop across the rest of the Gospel. The Greek word order, "with us God" rather than "God with us," figures in the disagreement: several commentators who resist the stronger reading argue the phrase functions adverbially, "with us is God," rather than as a predication identifying Jesus as God outright.↑
Usually god
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — reads the clause in the fullest sense, that Jesus as God's Son is also God himself among his people
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — gives a five-point argument against reading the clause as an ontological "Jesus is God" claim
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — reads the phrase adverbially, "with us is God," denying a full incarnational intent while affirming a unique divine presence
  • Carson (EBC) — argues for the strong reading from Isaiah's own theology, linking the name to Isaiah 9:6's "Mighty God"
  • Kupp (Matthew's Emmanuel) — reframes the question as functional rather than ontological, calling Jesus the agent of God's presence rather than adjudicating a "Son of God" claim directly

kuriou κύριος ranges from an ordinary term of respectful address ("sir," "master") through a disciple's title for a living teacher to the full divine name. In the Septuagint it is the standard reverential substitute for the unpronounced Hebrew Tetragrammaton. When the New Testament quotes an Old Testament passage that names it, the Greek text carries the same word, κύριος, with no orthographic marker distinguishing it from the word's ordinary human sense.↑
Usually lord, master, sir
Sources
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — states κύριος is used "in LXX (subst.) chiefly for [the divine name], also for בַּעַל, אָדוֹן," cataloguing the full range from genitive-of-possession ("master of the house") through vocative address ("sir") to the full divine title
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — "supreme in authority, i.e. controller; by implication, Master (as a respectful title)"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — cautions against over-reading an anarthrous κύριος as inherently a divine title, noting Psalm 110:1's "my lord" is ordinary court address to a king
  • France (NICNT) — on Matthew's citation of Isaiah 40:3, applying to Jesus a text whose κύριος names God himself constitutes "a remarkable christological claim"
  • Hengel (Studies in Early Christology) — traces how Psalm 110:1's double κύριος became the primary vehicle for the earliest church's confession of Jesus's exalted status

A minority reading takes the imperfect's own aspect as suggesting relations began only afterward, a reading directly rejected elsewhere in the commentary literature.↑
