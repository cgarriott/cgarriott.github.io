a
pneumati Pneuma spans a range from ordinary wind or breath to the animating principle in a person, an incorporeal spirit or demon, and the divine Spirit. Greek grammar does not resolve which sense is meant in a given case. The presence or absence of the article is sometimes read as marking the divine Spirit specifically, though this pattern is not applied consistently and remains disputed.↑
Usually spirit, ghost, wind
Sources
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — base sense "a current of air, i.e. breath (blast) or a breeze," extending by analogy to human soul, angel, demon, or God's own Spirit
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — five-sense entry, running from wind and breath through vital principle, disposition and incorporeal being to Holy Spirit, where the article's presence "as a rule" marks the Spirit as Person/Divine Power versus an anarthrous "operation, influence, or gift"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) and Davies and Allison (ICC) — both argue, independently, that "ἐκ πνεύματος ἁγίου" denotes divine creative power rather than a paternal/male generative role, Nolland via Septuagint idiom, Davies and Allison via grammatical gender (Greek πνεῦμα is neuter, Hebrew רוּחַ is feminine)
  • SBL Greek New Testament — πνεῦμα ἅγιον is anarthrous at Matthew 1:18, 1:20 and 3:11 but articular at 12:32 and 28:19
  • Blass, Debrunner and Funk (A Greek Grammar of the New Testament) — the article drops after a preposition and by assimilation to an anarthrous noun, which covers all three anarthrous occurrences
b
basileia βασιλεία covers both the act of ruling and the territory ruled. Ninety-nine of its hundred and sixty-two New Testament occurrences take a genitive of God or of the heavens. Most commentators hold that the sense there is the ruling rather than the realm, and they divide over whether English "kingdom", now a concrete noun, should give way to "kingship", "reign" or "dominion".↑
Usually kingdom, kingship
Sources
  • Danker (A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament) — files the word under the act of ruling, and warns that "kingdom of God" is often understood as a royal realm with the reigning activity diluted out of it
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — makes "sovereignty, royal power, dominion" the proper sense and the territory ruled a metonymy from it
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — the classical entry runs the other way, leading with "kingdom, dominion" and giving the kingly office separately
  • France (NICNT) — "This dynamic sense is now better conveyed by an abstract noun such as 'kingship' or 'sovereignty' rather than by 'kingdom,' which has become in general usage a concrete noun"
  • Boring (The New Interpreter's Bible) — "'kingdom' is a verbal noun whose primary connotation is not territory but ruling power, 'reign' or 'rule' often being the best translation"
  • Marcus (Anchor), Stein (BECNT), Turner (BECNT) and Guelich (The Sermon on the Mount) — the ruling rather than the realm, Marcus printing "the dominion of God has come near" in his own translation
  • Nolland (NIGTC), Marshall (NIGTC) and Carter (Matthew and the Margins) — print "royal rule", "kingly rule" and "empire" in their own translations
  • Sanders (Jesus and Judaism) — keeps "kingdom", his charge of "evading the obvious by the semantic device of denying that by 'kingdom' Jesus meant 'kingdom'" aimed by his own footnote at the readings that make the word a symbol, not at the reign-not-realm rendering
  • Dodd (The Parables of the Kingdom) and Meier (A Marginal Jew) — with Sanders, retaining the word while granting what it suggests to a reader
  • Aalen ("'Reign' and 'House' in the Kingdom of God in the Gospels," NTS) and Ladd (JBL), cited in Danker — the standing dissent, a house or community of God and a realm, against the ruling sense
  • Dalman (The Words of Jesus), cited in Dodd — the Semitic noun behind it, "as related to God always means 'kingly rule' and never 'kingdom'"

Usually righteousness
Sources
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — glosses δίκαιος "equitable (in character or act)," δικαιοσύνη "equity... specially (Christian) justification," spanning both senses
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — a two-tier entry, a broad sense "conformity to the Divine will in purpose, thought, and action" and a narrower sense "justice"
  • Przybylski (Righteousness in Matthew and His World of Thought) — argues Matthew's usage is uniformly ethical, "proper conduct before God," against a Pauline forensic-gift reading
  • Hagner (WBC) — an opposing view, that some occurrences (5:6, 6:33) denote eschatological "justice" or gift rather than ethical performance, explicitly against Przybylski
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — favors the "conduct" reading at 5:10, listing allied scholars (Barth, Fenton, Gundry, Luz, Przybylski, Strecker, Reumann, Ziesler) against dissenter Giesen

theon θεός ranges from a generic term for deity, one among many, to the proper-name-like designation of the God of Israel, usually marked by the article. An anarthrous predicate use is often read as qualitative, describing nature rather than identity, a distinction with no exact English equivalent. The word also applies, as in phrases like "son of God," to referents other than one unique divine being.↑
Usually god
Sources
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — "a deity, especially (with G3588 (ὁ)) the supreme Divinity", its own cross-reference there being to the article
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — distinguishes polytheistic use, anarthrous use (including as a qualitative predicate, "when the nature and character rather than the person of God is meant"), and the more frequent articular use
  • Harner ("Qualitative Anarthrous Predicate Nouns: Mark 15:39 and John 1:1," JBL) — argues anarthrous preverbal predicate θεός is typically qualitative, describing nature rather than identity
  • Colwell ("A Definite Rule for the Use of the Article in the Greek New Testament," JBL) — establishes that definite preverbal predicate nouns regularly drop the article in Koine Greek
  • Wallace (Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics) — refines Harner's rule, and treats passive-plus-predicate constructions of the they-will-be-called-sons shape as expressing a subset relation rather than strict identity

ponēron πονηρός runs from a hard day to the Evil One. Abbott-Smith derives it from πονέω, to toil, and files three of its New Testament occurrences under that older sense of toilsome or painful -- the days of Ephesians 5:16, the day of Ephesians 6:13, the sore of Revelation 16:2 -- before the senses of worthless and then actively wicked. The sharper difficulty is grammatical. In the genitive and dative singular the masculine and neuter are spelled alike, so "rescue us from the balesome" at Matthew 6:13 is either a power or a person, and nothing in the form decides it. Of the seventeen versions consulted here, nine print "the evil one" at that verse and seven print "evil"; the Vulgate's a malo is as undecidable as the Greek. One version prints both: the RSV of the Ignatius edition has "deliver us from evil" at 6:13 and "comes from the Evil One" at 5:37, for the same construction.↑
Usually evil, wicked, wicked one
Sources
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — derives the word from πονέω, to toil, and runs it from oppressed by toils and toilsome, painful through bad, worthless to the ethical sense, taking the singular at Matthew 5:37 and 6:13 as Satan and citing Lightfoot for the argument
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — the classical range runs from "in bad case, in sorry plight, useless, good-for-nothing" of an animal or a diet to the moral sense of knavish, and includes a political use of the baser sort against the καλοὶ κἀγαθοί
  • Danker (A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament) — glosses the substantive as "the evil one=the devil (who is not defined as a sinner but as one who is morally destructive)", and names ancient and modern readers on both sides of the gender at Matthew 5:37 and 6:13
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — takes the word as hurtful, evil in effect or influence, against κακός, "which refers rather to essential character", and records "masculine (singular) the devil, or (plural) sinners"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — reads the word abstractly at Matthew 6:13, since "πονηρός ('evil') is that which harms" and "Natural disaster is as much an 'evil' as is murder"
  • France (NICNT) — "the genitive form is the same in the masculine and neuter", so either reading stands, and the traditional rendering "would come to the same thing, but with a less personal understanding of the conflict"
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — follow the Eastern liturgies in reading the masculine, then take that very reading as a mark of lateness, since "'the evil one' never equals Satan in Hebrew or Aramaic literature. On the other hand, 'the evil one' does belong to the vocabulary of the church"
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — argues for the neuter from the Matthean and wider New Testament usage, reporting that "From ancient times it has been debated whether 'evil' in this final petition is to be understood as masculine or neuter"
  • Betz (Hermeneia) — traces the division to the early church, most Eastern fathers taking the devil and the Western fathers the neuter apart from Tertullian, and holds the language of prayer ambivalent enough that too sharp a distinction between the genders may not be advisable
  • Chase (The Lord's Prayer in the Early Church) — a chapter-length case for the personal reading, built on an antithesis between the Father and the devil running through the successive petitions
  • NRSVue, HCSB, NIV, NLT, NET and the Orthodox Study Bible — print the evil one at Matthew 6:13, against evil in the MacArthur Study Bible, the Ignatius Bible, ESV and KJV
  • ESV, NIV, NLT, HCSB, MacArthur Study Bible, Ignatius Bible and The Jewish Annotated New Testament — seven footnotes at Matthew 6:13, each offering the reading its own text declines

kosmou κόσμος means an ordered arrangement, and everything it names in the New Testament follows from that: the ordered universe, the inhabited earth, humanity, the present world-system set against God, and, at 1 Peter 3:3, a woman's outward adornment. The senses are not neutral. God so loved the kosmos (John 3:16), and do not love the kosmos (1 John 2:15), are the same word under contrary commands, and a translator who writes "world" in both places has already decided that one of them means something else.↑
Usually world, adorning
Sources
  • Danker (A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament) — the entry opens at "that which serves to beautify through decoration, adornment, adorning" and reaches the universe only in its third sense, after "condition of orderliness, orderly arrangement, order"
  • Danker (A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament) — a further sense in which "the world, and everything that belongs to it, appears as that which is hostile to God", found chiefly in Paul and the Johannine writings
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — the classical sense of order, from Homer's "in order, duly" through good behaviour and a state's constitution, with the philosophical world-order dated first to Pythagoras or Parmenides
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — the range from order and adornment through the universe to the inhabited world and its people
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — makes "orderly arrangement" the root sense and the world an implication drawn from it
  • Genesis 2:1 (Septuagint) — καὶ πᾶς ὁ κόσμος αὐτῶν closes the creation account, the word standing for the host of heaven and earth rather than for the world itself
  • Barrett (The Gospel According to St John) — the Johannine word is "not the sum total of creation but the organized and responsible world"
  • Brown (The Gospel According to John) — counts 78 of the New Testament's 185 occurrences in the Gospel of John, and holds the world there oriented toward evil and dominated by it rather than evil in itself
  • Keener (The Gospel of John) — sets the universe sense aside as outside John's usage, and reads the command not to love the world beside God's love for it as one tension inside a single term
  • Michaels (WBC) — at 1 Peter 3:3 warns against confusing "the specialized use of κόσμος here as 'adornment'" with its commoner meaning of world
  • KJV, HCSB and NIV — divide at 1 Peter 3:3 between "adorning" and "beauty" for the one noun
  • NRSVue — turns that noun into a verb, "[d]o not adorn yourselves outwardly by braiding your hair", so the word itself drops out of the verse
  • John 1:10 — one verse using the word in three of its senses in succession

nomon νόμος is the Greek word behind "the law," and it does not hold still. It names the Torah, the Mosaic covenant whole, a particular commandment, the Hebrew scriptures as a body, and, in Romans 7 and 8, something closer to a principle or governing power, a lege of the mind and a lege of peccancy at war in the same person. Paul stretches it across all of these inside a single argument, and whether a given occurrence means Torah or something wider is exactly what Christians have divided over since the Reformation. Printing "law" everywhere settles that silently.↑
Usually law, principle

paradōi One Greek verb covers handing something over, delivering a person up to the authorities, betraying, entrusting, risking, and handing on a teaching to those who come after. English cannot say it without choosing a particle -- hand over, hand on, hand down, give up -- and each choice decides the sense. So CNT prints the verb bare, as hand, and the noun as handdown. The cost of not doing so is visible at 1 Corinthians 11:23, where Paul uses the word twice in one sentence -- what he handed to the Corinthians, and the night on which the kyrios was handed -- and where every English translation splits it into two unrelated words, so that a reader cannot see that the word used of Judas is the word used of passing on what was received.↑
Usually delivered up, delivered, betray
Sources
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — files five senses under a single entry, running from "to give or hand over to another" through committing, delivering up to prison or judgment, "to hand down, hand on or deliver verbally", and permitting
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — the classical entry has the same reach, and treats betrayal as a shading of surrender rather than a sense of its own, "with collateral notion of treachery, betray"
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — defines the verb neutrally as "to surrender, i.e yield up, intrust, transmit", and gives the King James renderings as "betray, bring forth, cast, commit, deliver (up), give (over, up), hazard, put in prison, recommend"
  • Danker (A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament) — separates handing over, entrusting for care, and passing on tradition into three numbered senses, and doubts at 1 Corinthians 11:23 that Paul is "even thinking of the action taken against Jesus by Judas much less interpreting it as betrayal"
  • Marcus (Anchor) — "The basic meaning of paradidonai is to transfer something or someone into the possession of another, usually without any nuance of treachery", and the negative colouring in the Gospels follows from the association with Judas
  • Marcus (Anchor) — at Mark 1:14 no agent is named, so the verb may carry both a human arrest and God's own delivering up of his servant, as at Isaiah 53:6 and 12
  • Brown (The Death of the Messiah) — reports the tendency to render the verb as betray, and holds that such a rendering obscures the parallel between Judas's act and the agency of others in the passion narrative
  • Popkes (Christus Traditus), cited in Brown (The Death of the Messiah) — sorts the passion uses by whether Jesus is given over by God, by human beings, or by himself
  • Fitzmyer (Romans) — counts four nuances in Paul alone, the vicarious handing over of Romans 4:25 and 8:32, handing over to degradation at 1:24, 26 and 28, handing over to another master at 6:17, and passing on a tradition at 1 Corinthians 11:2 and 15:3
  • Fitzmyer (First Corinthians) — holds Paul "scarcely referring" to Judas at 11:23, and reads the passive as either divine or middle
  • Thiselton (NIGTC) — sets four published renderings of the one clause against each other, betrayed, arrested, delivered up and handed over, and follows Hays in holding that the handing over by God eclipses the allusion to Judas
  • Fee (NICNT) — grants that the verb "is just ambiguous enough so that it could mean 'handed over'", but holds its first reference is the treachery of Judas
  • Bauckham (Jesus and the Eyewitnesses) — reads the verb at 1 Corinthians 11:2 and 15:3 as the fixed term for formal transmission, current in the Hellenistic schools and in Jewish Greek before Paul

ponērou πονηρός runs from a hard day to the Evil One. Abbott-Smith derives it from πονέω, to toil, and files three of its New Testament occurrences under that older sense of toilsome or painful -- the days of Ephesians 5:16, the day of Ephesians 6:13, the sore of Revelation 16:2 -- before the senses of worthless and then actively wicked. The sharper difficulty is grammatical. In the genitive and dative singular the masculine and neuter are spelled alike, so "rescue us from the balesome" at Matthew 6:13 is either a power or a person, and nothing in the form decides it. Of the seventeen versions consulted here, nine print "the evil one" at that verse and seven print "evil"; the Vulgate's a malo is as undecidable as the Greek. One version prints both: the RSV of the Ignatius edition has "deliver us from evil" at 6:13 and "comes from the Evil One" at 5:37, for the same construction.↑
Usually evil, wicked, wicked one

  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — derives the word from πονέω, to toil, and runs it from oppressed by toils and toilsome, painful through bad, worthless to the ethical sense, taking the singular at Matthew 5:37 and 6:13 as Satan and citing Lightfoot for the argument
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — the classical range runs from "in bad case, in sorry plight, useless, good-for-nothing" of an animal or a diet to the moral sense of knavish, and includes a political use of the baser sort against the καλοὶ κἀγαθοί

  • Nolland (NIGTC) — reads the word abstractly at Matthew 6:13, since "πονηρός ('evil') is that which harms" and "Natural disaster is as much an 'evil' as is murder"
  • France (NICNT) — "the genitive form is the same in the masculine and neuter", so either reading stands, and the traditional rendering "would come to the same thing, but with a less personal understanding of the conflict"
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — follow the Eastern liturgies in reading the masculine, then take that very reading as a mark of lateness, since "'the evil one' never equals Satan in Hebrew or Aramaic literature. On the other hand, 'the evil one' does belong to the vocabulary of the church"

  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — the classical range runs from "in bad case, in sorry plight, useless, good-for-nothing" of an animal or a diet to the moral sense of knavish, and includes a political use of the baser sort against the καλοὶ κἀγαθοί
  • Danker (A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament) — glosses the substantive as "the evil one=the devil (who is not defined as a sinner but as one who is morally destructive)", and names ancient and modern readers on both sides of the gender at Matthew 5:37 and 6:13
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — takes the word as hurtful, evil in effect or influence, against κακός, "which refers rather to essential character", and records "masculine (singular) the devil, or (plural) sinners"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — reads the word abstractly at Matthew 6:13, since "πονηρός ('evil') is that which harms" and "Natural disaster is as much an 'evil' as is murder"
  • France (NICNT) — "the genitive form is the same in the masculine and neuter", so either reading stands, and the traditional rendering "would come to the same thing, but with a less personal understanding of the conflict"
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — follow the Eastern liturgies in reading the masculine, then take that very reading as a mark of lateness, since "'the evil one' never equals Satan in Hebrew or Aramaic literature. On the other hand, 'the evil one' does belong to the vocabulary of the church"
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — argues for the neuter from the Matthean and wider New Testament usage, reporting that "From ancient times it has been debated whether 'evil' in this final petition is to be understood as masculine or neuter"
  • Betz (Hermeneia) — traces the division to the early church, most Eastern fathers taking the devil and the Western fathers the neuter apart from Tertullian, and holds the language of prayer ambivalent enough that too sharp a distinction between the genders may not be advisable
  • Chase (The Lord's Prayer in the Early Church) — a chapter-length case for the personal reading, built on an antithesis between the Father and the devil running through the successive petitions
  • NRSVue, HCSB, NIV, NLT, NET and the Orthodox Study Bible — print the evil one at Matthew 6:13, against evil in the MacArthur Study Bible, the Ignatius Bible, ESV and KJV
  • ESV, NIV, NLT, HCSB, MacArthur Study Bible, Ignatius Bible and The Jewish Annotated New Testament — seven footnotes at Matthew 6:13, each offering the reading its own text declines

adelphous ἀδελφός is a brother by birth. It also covers wider kin, a compatriot, and a member of a religious association. The Greek Septuagint calls Abraham and his nephew Lot adelphoi. Whether that range reaches cousin in the New Testament is disputed, and the men named as Jesus's adelphoi are the case in question. Greek had a separate word for cousin and the New Testament uses it once. The masculine plural can take in women, and the versions divide over printing sisters.↑
Usually brother
Sources
  • Danker (A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament) — holds the Septuagint passages "do not establish the mng. 'cousin' for" the word, showing only that it renders the Hebrew loosely for male relatives of various degrees
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — "the view of Jerome, which makes ἀ. equivalent to ἀνεψιός, is inconsistent with Greek usage"
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — enters kinsman as its own sense citing Genesis 13:8, and enters the word for a first cousin separately
  • Meier (A Marginal Jew) — the siblings reading on philological grounds, since the Pauline tradition "knew perfectly well the word for 'cousin'" and used it at Colossians 4:10, so "the most probable opinion is that the brothers and sisters of Jesus were his siblings"
  • France (NICNT) and Carson (EBC) — with Meier, France holding that "It is dogma, not exegesis, which suggests that they were either cousins or Joseph's children by a previous marriage"
  • Mitch and Sri (CCSS), the Ignatius Bible and the Orthodox Study Bible — the wider reading, that "The Hebrew language had no word for cousin, and it was common to use the word brother to describe cousins or other males in the extended family"
  • Hooker (The Gospel According to Saint Mark) — asserts the wide range while declining the conclusion drawn from it, the word usable "of any male relative" though Mark "knows nothing" of the belief it is made to serve
  • Glossa Ordinaria — the traditional identification stated outright, "Fratres Domini sunt consobrini ejus, filii Mariæ materteræ ejus"
  • Danker (A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament), France (NICNT) and Fee (NICNT) — on the inclusive plural, the lexicon's own introductory essay, signed by Bauer, saying "There is no longer any doubt in my mind that ἀδελφοί can mean 'brothers and sisters' in any number"
  • Thiselton (NIGTC) — grants that sense and holds it "escapes precise translation by any single English word or phrase"
  • Septuagint — Genesis 13:8 and 14:14 call Abraham and Lot adelphoi where 11:27 makes Lot his brother's son, and Leviticus 10:4 names men as sons of Aaron's father's brother and then calls the dead "your adelphoi"
