CNT — Corey's Non-Translation
Theological: always untranslated Crux: untranslated here Variant: editions disagree

:

Τότε ὁ Ἰησοῦς ἀνήχθη εἰς τὴν ἔρημον ὑπὸ τοῦ πνεύματος πειρασθῆναι ὑπὸ τοῦ διαβόλου

Then Jesus was led up into the wilderness by the pneumaa, to be peiraziedb by the diabolosc.

Notes

a
pneumatos Pneuma spans a range from ordinary wind or breath to the animating principle in a person, an incorporeal spirit or demon, and the divine Spirit. Greek grammar does not resolve which sense is meant in a given case. The presence or absence of the article is sometimes read as marking the divine Spirit specifically, though this pattern is not applied consistently and remains disputed.↑
Usually spirit, ghost, wind
Sources
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — base sense "a current of air, i.e. breath (blast) or a breeze," extending by analogy to human soul, angel, demon, or God's own Spirit
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — five-sense entry, running from wind and breath through vital principle, disposition and incorporeal being to Holy Spirit, where the article's presence "as a rule" marks the Spirit as Person/Divine Power versus an anarthrous "operation, influence, or gift"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) and Davies and Allison (ICC) — both argue, independently, that "ἐκ πνεύματος ἁγίου" denotes divine creative power rather than a paternal/male generative role, Nolland via Septuagint idiom, Davies and Allison via grammatical gender (Greek πνεῦμα is neuter, Hebrew רוּחַ is feminine)
  • SBL Greek New Testament — πνεῦμα ἅγιον is anarthrous at Matthew 1:18, 1:20 and 3:11 but articular at 12:32 and 28:19
  • Blass, Debrunner and Funk (A Greek Grammar of the New Testament) — the article drops after a preposition and by assimilation to an anarthrous noun, which covers all three anarthrous occurrences
b
peirasthēnai One Greek root that English splits into testing and tempting, and only the second carries a verdict of solicitation to evil. The commentators converge on a double agency rather than choosing between the two, and the lexicon files this verse under the hostile sense while allowing both.↑
Usually tempted, testing, tried
Sources
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — "Satan tempts and, thereby, God tests"
  • France (NICNT) — the devil's intention to tempt "is subsumed under God's good purpose to 'test' his Son"
  • Turner (BECNT), citing Calvin — "[t]he Father's aim is to accredit Jesus, the devil's to discredit Jesus"
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — independently the same, and supply a rabbinic parallel of Satan trading Scripture with Abraham
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — splits the verb into a good sense of trials sent by God and a bad sense of temptation, citing this verse for the second
c
diabolou An ordinary adjective meaning slanderous before it is a title, and as a noun a slanderer or accuser. The Septuagint uses it for the Hebrew word for an adversary. Matthew uses this word six times and the Hebrew-derived name four, and the second appears only in Jesus's own speech. διάβολος is an ordinary Greek adjective meaning slanderous, and as a noun a slanderer or accuser. The Septuagint uses it for the Hebrew word for an adversary in Job, Zechariah, 1 Chronicles and Psalm 109, where the figure stands as an accuser in a court. Three of its thirty-seven New Testament occurrences are the plural adjective, and there not one of seventeen versions reads "devil". Whether the word is a title or a name is disputed.↑
Usually devil, slanderers, false accusers
Sources
  • France (NICNT) — the two names are "virtually interchangeable in the gospel tradition", despite the different roots
  • The Jewish Annotated New Testament — glosses it as the equivalent of the Hebrew, "accuser"
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — "Matthew, who himself prefers διάβολος, leaves σατανᾶς in sayings of Jesus"
  • Revelation 12:9 — equates the two names, where Matthew never does
