καὶ λέγει αὐτῷ· Εἰ υἱὸς εἶ τοῦ θεοῦ βάλε σεαυτὸν κάτω· γέγραπται γὰρ ὅτι Τοῖς ἀγγέλοις αὐτοῦ ἐντελεῖται περὶ σοῦ καὶ ἐπὶ χειρῶν ἀροῦσίν σε, μήποτε προσκόψῃς πρὸς λίθον τὸν πόδα σου.

and says to him, "Eia you are filiusb of theosc, throw yourself down; for it stands written that 'To his angels he will command concerning you, and on hands they will bear you, lest ever you strike against a stone your foot.'"

Notes

a
Ei The identical clause to 4:3, repeated verbatim. See the note there.↑
Usually whether, though
Sources
  • Wallace (Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics) — a supposition assumed for argument, not "since"
  • Boyer ("First Class Conditions: What Do They Mean?", GTJ) — the construction implies nothing about reality
  • Chrysostom (Homilies on Matthew) — genuine uncertainty on the devil's part
  • Matthew 4:3 — the same clause, two verses earlier
b
huios The psalm quoted in support, Psalm 90:11-12 in the Septuagint, promises angelic protection to whoever shelters in the Most High, and it names no son and no messiah. What is disputed is the move from that addressee to this one. Readings of it range from an argument from the lesser case to the greater, through a demand for a public sign, to an accusation of blasphemy answerable by stoning. One commentary reads the temptation as no different in shape from any facing other sons of God.↑
Usually son, child, foal
Sources
  • Psalm 90:11-12 (Septuagint) — the promise is addressed to the one who dwells in the shelter of the Most High, and no son stands anywhere in the psalm
  • France (NICNT) — the passage "is addressed to all who have chosen to 'live in the shelter of the Most High'", and the argument runs from there to "God's special Son"
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — "Ps 91 did not receive a messianic interpretation in Judaism", and the words are best read as asking how the Son should exercise his powers
  • Chrysostom (Homilies on Matthew) — the citation fails on its subject and not only on its application, "this was not so much as spoken concerning the Lord"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — the temptation's "fundamental shape is not different from any temptation which faces other 'sons of God'"
  • Gerhardsson (The Testing of God's Son) — no onlookers are in view, and what is tested is the relation between God and the Son alone
  • Boring (The New Interpreter's Bible) — against them, a sensational demonstration of sonship before an audience, and the silence about spectators does not settle it
  • Hyldahl ("Die Versuchung auf der Zinne des Tempels," ST), cited and rejected in Hagner (WBC) — the leap as a demanded submission to stoning for the blasphemy of claiming sonship, where survival is the proof
  • Albright and Mann (Anchor) — the scene turns on Israel's constant temptation to presume on sonship, which the prophets attest
c
theou θεός ranges from a generic term for deity, one among many, to the proper-name-like designation of the God of Israel, usually marked by the article. An anarthrous predicate use is often read as qualitative, describing nature rather than identity, a distinction with no exact English equivalent. The word also applies, as in phrases like "son of God," to referents other than one unique divine being.↑
Usually god
Sources
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — "a deity, especially (with G3588 (ὁ)) the supreme Divinity", its own cross-reference there being to the article
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — distinguishes polytheistic use, anarthrous use (including as a qualitative predicate, "when the nature and character rather than the person of God is meant"), and the more frequent articular use
  • Harner ("Qualitative Anarthrous Predicate Nouns: Mark 15:39 and John 1:1," JBL) — argues anarthrous preverbal predicate θεός is typically qualitative, describing nature rather than identity
  • Colwell ("A Definite Rule for the Use of the Article in the Greek New Testament," JBL) — establishes that definite preverbal predicate nouns regularly drop the article in Koine Greek
  • Wallace (Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics) — refines Harner's rule, and treats passive-plus-predicate constructions of the they-will-be-called-sons shape as expressing a subset relation rather than strict identity
