CNT — Corey's Non-Translation
Theological: always untranslated Crux: untranslated here Variant: editions disagree

:

τότε λέγει αὐτῷ ὁ Ἰησοῦς· Ὕπαγε Σατανᾶ γέγραπται γάρ· Κύριον τὸν θεόν σου προσκυνήσεις καὶ αὐτῷ μόνῳ λατρεύσεις

Then Jesus says to him, "Hupagiea, Satanasb; for it stands written, 'Kuriosc your theosd you shall proskyniee, and to him alone you shall latrief.'"

Notes

a
Hupage The imperative is an ordinary verb of going, used seventeen times in Matthew, most often to send someone off on an errand. Across seventeen versions the renderings run from a bare "Go" to "Get out of here." Some manuscripts add "behind me" after it, matching word for word the rebuke Jesus later gives Peter at 16:23, and editors treat the addition as a copyist's assimilation to that verse.↑
Usually go, go away, get
Sources
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — a later present tense meaning simply to go, set against the verb for coming, and citing this verse as its first example
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — an intransitive going away or departing, grouping the imperative here with 8:13 and 20:14
  • France (NICNT) — the imperative usually sends someone off on a task, and the edge here comes from the vocative that follows: "he is sending his adversary packing"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — renders it "Get away" while noting the verb literally means depart, and reads the added words as early evidence that the link with 16:23 was recognized
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — "the devil does not follow behind Jesus as a disciple", so the added words fit the later verse and not this one
  • Metzger (A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament) — the shorter reading at its highest confidence grade, since nothing would have prompted a copyist to drop the two words
  • Chrysostom (Homilies on Matthew) — a rebuke made without vehemence, the words having "something of command rather than of rebuke"
b
Satana The word transliterates a Hebrew common noun for an adversary or an accuser. At Job 1:6 and Zechariah 3:1 the Septuagint translates that noun instead, using the same Greek word the narrator has used of the tempter throughout this scene, while at 1 Kings 11:14 it carries the Hebrew over untranslated. Where Matthew uses the word outside direct address it takes the definite article. One version alone prints "Accuser."↑
Usually satan
Sources
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — the Hebrew word for an adversary or opponent, and also an accuser, rendered in the Septuagint by the word for a slanderer
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — glosses it as the adversary, and traces the inflected Greek form to the Aramaic
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — the accuser, from an Aramaic form already carrying "the definite affix"
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — a name for the devil, and an appellation that became current around the turn of the era, derived from the Hebrew for adversary
  • Hart — the one version to translate rather than transliterate, "Be gone, Accuser", footnoting it as "'Satan,' which is to say, 'prosecutor,' 'accuser,' 'arraigner.'"
  • Job 1:6 and Zechariah 3:1 (Septuagint) — the noun rendered by the narrator's own word for the tempter, against 1 Kings 11:14, where it is left in Hebrew
c
Kurion κύριος ranges from an ordinary term of respectful address ("sir," "master") through a disciple's title for a living teacher to the full divine name. In the Septuagint it is the standard reverential substitute for the unpronounced Hebrew Tetragrammaton. When the New Testament quotes an Old Testament passage that names it, the Greek text carries the same word, κύριος, with no orthographic marker distinguishing it from the word's ordinary human sense.↑
Usually lord, master, sir
Sources
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — states κύριος is used "in LXX (subst.) chiefly for [the divine name], also for [Baal, Adon]," cataloguing the full range from genitive-of-possession ("master of the house") through vocative address ("sir") to the full divine title
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — "supreme in authority, i.e. (as noun) controller; by implication, Master (as a respectful title)"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — cautions against over-reading an anarthrous κύριος as inherently a divine title, noting Psalm 110:1's "my lord" is ordinary court address to a king
  • France (NICNT) — on Matthew's citation of Isaiah 40:3, applying to Jesus a text whose κύριος names God himself constitutes "a remarkable christological claim"
d
theon θεός ranges from a generic term for deity, one among many, to the proper-name-like designation of the God of Israel, usually marked by the article. An anarthrous predicate use is often read as qualitative, describing nature rather than identity, a distinction with no exact English equivalent. The word also applies, as in phrases like "son of God," to referents other than one unique divine being.↑
Usually god
Sources
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — "a deity, especially (with G3588 (ὁ)) the supreme Divinity", its own cross-reference there being to the article
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — distinguishes polytheistic use, anarthrous use (including as a qualitative predicate, "when the nature and character rather than the person of God is meant"), and the more frequent articular use
  • Harner ("Qualitative Anarthrous Predicate Nouns: Mark 15:39 and John 1:1," JBL) — argues anarthrous preverbal predicate θεός is typically qualitative, describing nature rather than identity
  • Colwell ("A Definite Rule for the Use of the Article in the Greek New Testament," JBL) — establishes that definite preverbal predicate nouns regularly drop the article in Koine Greek
  • Wallace (Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics) — refines Harner's rule, and treats passive-plus-predicate constructions of the they-will-be-called-sons shape as expressing a subset relation rather than strict identity
e
proskunēseis προσκυνέω denotes full prostration before a superior, a gesture Greek usage associated with subjects bowing before Persian kings. The same verb covers ordinary court homage to a human ruler and the cultic worship of a god. Koine grammar supplies no separate construction that reliably marks which sense is meant. Context alone decides between the two.↑
Usually worship, homage, kneeling down
Sources
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — etymology πρός + a derivative of κύων ("dog"), "to fawn or crouch to... prostrate oneself in homage"
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — splits New Testament usage into two senses, of God, Christ and supra-mundane beings, and of homage to human superiors as in classical usage, placing Matthew 2:2, 2:8 and 2:11 in the latter alongside Matthew 4:9
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — names the "Oriental fashion of prostrating oneself before kings and superiors," the same courtly sense used of subjects bowing to Persian kings in Herodotus
  • Matthew 18:26 — the same verb of a slave prostrating before a human master
  • Brown (The Birth of the Messiah) — proposes the verb's own sense may shift over the course of Matthew 2's narrative, from royal homage toward something less stable by 2:11
  • Turner (BECNT) and France (NICNT) — a live disagreement on whether Matthew 2:11's added prostration signals worship (Turner) or ordinary court prostration (France)
f
latreuseis The couplet sets two verbs of reverence four words apart. The first names a bodily gesture that may be offered to a human superior. The second is used in the Septuagint only of service to a god, and whether it is narrower still, naming priestly or cultic service in particular, is disputed. Sixteen of the seventeen versions render it "serve."↑
Usually serve, worship
Sources
  • Brown (The Birth of the Messiah) — the cultic reading, holding that the verb "most often carries a tone of religious or cultic service" against the more general one
  • Fitzmyer (Romans) — the same, holding the verb "reserved for the cult of Yahweh"
  • Beale (NIGTC) — the same restriction, argued from the Septuagint's own usage, where the verb "is used almost exclusively in contexts of worship"
  • Hahn (CCSS) — cites this verse as his own instance of the verb carrying the sense of divine worship
  • Schreiner (Romans, BECNT) — calls the verb "a cultic term that designates service in the gospel"; in his Revelation (BECNT) he groups this verse's own source text with the passages where fearing God and worshipping him are one, setting the verb beside douleuō and proskyneō as alternates for a single act, which is evidence for the general sense rather than the narrow one
  • Marshall (NIGTC) — against them, the priestly use "came to have a more general meaning"
  • Koester (Revelation, Anchor) — with Marshall, the verb "used for all who serve God in fear, love, obedience, and prayer"
  • Bock (Luke 1:1-9:50, BECNT) — also against the narrowing, the verb naming "the total service one gives to God, not just to the worship or sacrificial service that a faithful Jew would render in the temple or synagogue"
  • Glossa Ordinaria — argues from the restriction that the Spirit is God, naming the service owed to the Creator alone against the mutual service of charity
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — the core sense of working for hire, with a specialized religious sense of serving the gods
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — in the Septuagint always of service to a deity, glossing the New Testament use as both service and worship
