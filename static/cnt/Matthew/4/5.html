CNT — Corey's Non-Translation
Theological: always untranslated Crux: untranslated here Variant: editions disagree

:

Τότε παραλαμβάνει αὐτὸν ὁ διάβολος εἰς τὴν ἁγίαν πόλιν, καὶ ἔστησεν αὐτὸν ἐπὶ τὸ πτερύγιον τοῦ ἱεροῦ,

Then the diabolosa withtakesb him into the holy city, and stoodc him upon the pterugiond of the temple,

Notes

a
diabolos See the note at 4:1. The word occurs four times in this chapter. διάβολος is an ordinary Greek adjective meaning slanderous, and as a noun a slanderer or accuser. The Septuagint uses it for the Hebrew word for an adversary in Job, Zechariah, 1 Chronicles and Psalm 109, where the figure stands as an accuser in a court. Three of its thirty-seven New Testament occurrences are the plural adjective, and there not one of seventeen versions reads "devil". Whether the word is a title or a name is disputed.↑
Usually devil, slanderers, false accusers
Sources
  • Danker (A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament) — puts the ordinary sense first, pertaining to engagement in slander, and classes the other as a title of the principal transcendent evil being
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — charts the whole progression inside one entry, from "slanderous, backbiting" through the substantive slanderer to the Septuagint's enemy
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — splits the occurrences into "a slanderer, false accuser" and, "as chiefly in LXX, of Satan, the Accuser, the Devil"
  • Lesses ("Supernatural Beings," The Jewish Annotated New Testament) — dates the shift, since in Job "'Satan' is here a title, not a personal name" while "[i]n 1 Chronicles, likely from the fourth century bce, 'Satan' first appears as a proper name (21.1)"
  • Elliott (Anchor) — the word is "Used without an article and therefore virtually as a proper name"
  • Quinn (Anchor) — the reverse premise, "with the articular diabolos in the singular becoming a personal title"
  • Williams and Horrell (ICC) — reject the appeal to the article altogether, since "the inclusion of the article before διάβολος would actually support an attributive (not a substantival) function"
  • Aune (WBC) — neither and both, holding that the word and its Hebrew counterpart "are examples of such transitional proper names"
  • Johnson (Anchor), Marshall (ICC) and Mounce (WBC) — the plain adjective at the Pastorals' plural, Mounce that "in each of these cases it means 'slanderous'"
  • Septuagint — renders the Hebrew word for an adversary with this word at Job 1-2, Zechariah 3, 1 Chronicles 21:1 and Psalm 109:6, uses a different word at 1 Samuel 29:4 and 1 Kings 5:4, and transliterates it at 1 Kings 11:14
  • Hart — the one version of seventeen to translate rather than transliterate throughout, printing "the Slanderer" here against "the Accuser" for the Hebrew-derived word
b
paralambanei A present tense in past narrative, and the same verb given to Joseph in the birth narrative for taking the child and his mother into Egypt and back. Whether the relocation is physical or visionary is a real question, and everyone who addresses it leans visionary, with varying confidence.↑
Usually taken, received, took unto
Sources
  • France (NICNT) — visionary, since no actual mountain gives a view of all the kingdoms, comparing Ezekiel taken to Jerusalem while in Babylon
  • Turner (BECNT) — independently the same, "most likely describes a visionary experience rather than physical movement"
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — more hedged, calling the question unclear, and set beside it ancient parallels of literal teleportation
  • Blomberg (NAC) — traces the visionary reading to Theodore of Mopsuestia and the Antiochenes, endorsed by Calvin and defended by Ridderbos
  • Matthew 2:14 and 2:21 — the same verb, of Joseph taking the child and his mother, the pairing Nolland reads as an antithetical parallel to the devil here
c
estēsen The narrative uses seven verbs of the devil across this scene. Five are present tense. The two aorists are this one and the speech verb at 4:9, and those are exactly the two places where the Byzantine tradition and the Textus Receptus read a present instead. Two of seventeen versions keep a present for the first verb of this verse, and one of the two prints a present for the second as well.↑
Sources
  • Translators Amalgamated Greek New Testament — six of the eight editions collated read the aorist ἔστησεν, and the Textus Receptus and the Byzantine text read the present ἵστησιν
  • KJV — "setteth him on a pinnacle of the temple", a present at this verb and again at 4:9
  • Hart — "carries him off into the Holy City, and stood him upon the pinnacle of the Temple", which keeps the shift of tense
  • Wallace (Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics) — the historical present portrays a past event "vividly, as though the reader were in the midst of the scene as it unfolds"
  • Turner (BECNT) — counts more than eighty historical presents in Matthew, and lists 4:5, 4:8 and 4:11 among those outside speech
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — renders the historic present as a present in English, and holds these "come too thick and fast to mark specific points of emphasis, but they do create a sense of crescendo"
  • Metzger (A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament) — carries no entry at this verse, and his nearest Matthew entries stand at 3:16 and 4:10
d
pterugion A diminutive of the word for wing, so a little wing, and by extension an extremity or a projection. Which feature of the temple is meant has produced at least six proposals, and one lexicon registers the dispute without adjudicating it.↑
Usually pinnacle
Sources
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — records the dispute itself, "turret or battlement, or (as others) pointed roof, peak"
  • France (NICNT) — declines to choose, holding it "not very important to decide just which part of the actual Herodian temple was meant", and quotes Josephus on the giddying drop
  • Turner (BECNT) and Mitch and Sri (CCSS) — the temple complex's southeast corner, a portico overhanging the Kidron valley
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — agnostic, and alone reports Schwarz's argument from Syriac for a shoulder of the roof
  • Gerhardsson (The Testing of God's Son) — surveys the proposals from Jeremias onward, and argues the word is a deliberate pun anticipating the "wings" earlier in the psalm quoted in the next verse
  • Danker (A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament) — supplies Jeremias's own conclusion, the lintel or superstructure of a gate, with a reservation that Greek had a more natural word for that
  • Goulder (Midrash and Lection in Matthew) — a textual derivation instead, from Daniel's "pinnacle of abominations"
  • Hagner (WBC) — reports, while declining to endorse, Hyldahl's proposal that the leap is a demanded submission to stoning for blasphemy
