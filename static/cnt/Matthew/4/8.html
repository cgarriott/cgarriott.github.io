Theological: always untranslated Crux: untranslated here Variant: editions disagree

:

Πάλιν παραλαμβάνει αὐτὸν ὁ διάβολος εἰς ὄρος ὑψηλὸν λίαν, καὶ δείκνυσιν αὐτῷ πάσας τὰς βασιλείας τοῦ κόσμου καὶ τὴν δόξαν αὐτῶν

Again the diabolosa takes him into a mountain exceedingly high, and shows him all the basileiaib of the kosmosc and their glory,

Notes

a
diabolos See the note at 4:1. διάβολος is an ordinary Greek adjective meaning slanderous, and as a noun a slanderer or accuser. The Septuagint uses it for the Hebrew word for an adversary in Job, Zechariah, 1 Chronicles and Psalm 109, where the figure stands as an accuser in a court. Three of its thirty-seven New Testament occurrences are the plural adjective, and there not one of seventeen versions reads "devil". Whether the word is a title or a name is disputed.↑
Usually devil, slanderers, false accusers
Sources
  • Danker (A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament) — puts the ordinary sense first, pertaining to engagement in slander, and classes the other as a title of the principal transcendent evil being
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — charts the whole progression inside one entry, from "slanderous, backbiting" through the substantive slanderer to the Septuagint's enemy
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — splits the occurrences into "a slanderer, false accuser" and, "as chiefly in LXX, of Satan, the Accuser, the Devil"
  • Lesses ("Supernatural Beings," The Jewish Annotated New Testament) — dates the shift, since in Job "'Satan' is here a title, not a personal name" while "[i]n 1 Chronicles, likely from the fourth century bce, 'Satan' first appears as a proper name (21.1)"
  • Elliott (Anchor) — the word is "Used without an article and therefore virtually as a proper name"
  • Quinn (Anchor) — the reverse premise, "with the articular diabolos in the singular becoming a personal title"
  • Williams and Horrell (ICC) — reject the appeal to the article altogether, since "the inclusion of the article before διάβολος would actually support an attributive (not a substantival) function"
  • Aune (WBC) — neither and both, holding that the word and its Hebrew counterpart "are examples of such transitional proper names"
  • Johnson (Anchor), Marshall (ICC) and Mounce (WBC) — the plain adjective at the Pastorals' plural, Mounce that "in each of these cases it means 'slanderous'"
  • Septuagint — renders the Hebrew word for an adversary with this word at Job 1-2, Zechariah 3, 1 Chronicles 21:1 and Psalm 109:6, uses a different word at 1 Samuel 29:4 and 1 Kings 5:4, and transliterates it at 1 Kings 11:14
  • Hart — the one version of seventeen to translate rather than transliterate throughout, printing "the Slanderer" here against "the Accuser" for the Hebrew-derived word
b
basileias βασιλεία covers both the act of ruling and the territory ruled. Ninety-nine of its hundred and sixty-two New Testament occurrences take a genitive of God or of the heavens. Most commentators hold that the sense there is the ruling rather than the realm, and they divide over whether English "kingdom", now a concrete noun, should give way to "kingship", "reign" or "dominion".↑
Usually kingdom, kingship
Sources
  • Danker (A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament) — files the word under the act of ruling, and warns that "kingdom of God" is often understood as a royal realm with the reigning activity diluted out of it
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — makes "sovereignty, royal power, dominion" the proper sense and the territory ruled a metonymy from it
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — the classical entry runs the other way, leading with "kingdom, dominion" and giving the kingly office separately
  • France (NICNT) — "This dynamic sense is now better conveyed by an abstract noun such as 'kingship' or 'sovereignty' rather than by 'kingdom,' which has become in general usage a concrete noun"
  • Boring (The New Interpreter's Bible) — "'kingdom' is a verbal noun whose primary connotation is not territory but ruling power, 'reign' or 'rule' often being the best translation"
  • Marcus (Anchor), Stein (BECNT), Turner (BECNT) and Guelich (The Sermon on the Mount) — the ruling rather than the realm, Marcus printing "the dominion of God has come near" in his own translation
  • Nolland (NIGTC), Marshall (NIGTC) and Carter (Matthew and the Margins) — print "royal rule", "kingly rule" and "empire" in their own translations
  • Sanders (Jesus and Judaism) — keeps "kingdom", his charge of "evading the obvious by the semantic device of denying that by 'kingdom' Jesus meant 'kingdom'" aimed by his own footnote at the readings that make the word a symbol, not at the reign-not-realm rendering
  • Dodd (The Parables of the Kingdom) and Meier (A Marginal Jew) — with Sanders, retaining the word while granting what it suggests to a reader
  • Aalen ("'Reign' and 'House' in the Kingdom of God in the Gospels," NTS) and Ladd (JBL), cited in Danker — the standing dissent, a house or community of God and a realm, against the ruling sense
  • Dalman (The Words of Jesus), cited in Dodd — the Semitic noun behind it, "as related to God always means 'kingly rule' and never 'kingdom'"
c
kosmou κόσμος means an ordered arrangement, and everything it names in the New Testament follows from that: the ordered universe, the inhabited earth, humanity, the present world-system set against God, and, at 1 Peter 3:3, a woman's outward adornment. The senses are not neutral. God so loved the kosmos (John 3:16), and do not love the kosmos (1 John 2:15), are the same word under contrary commands, and a translator who writes "world" in both places has already decided that one of them means something else.↑
Usually world, adorning
Sources
  • Danker (A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament) — the entry opens at "that which serves to beautify through decoration, adornment, adorning" and reaches the universe only in its third sense, after "condition of orderliness, orderly arrangement, order"
  • Danker (A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament) — a further sense in which "the world, and everything that belongs to it, appears as that which is hostile to God", found chiefly in Paul and the Johannine writings
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — the classical sense of order, from Homer's "in order, duly" through good behaviour and a state's constitution, with the philosophical world-order dated first to Pythagoras or Parmenides
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — the range from order and adornment through the universe to the inhabited world and its people
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — makes "orderly arrangement" the root sense and the world an implication drawn from it
  • Genesis 2:1 (Septuagint) — καὶ πᾶς ὁ κόσμος αὐτῶν closes the creation account, the word standing for the host of heaven and earth rather than for the world itself
  • Barrett (The Gospel According to St John) — the Johannine word is "not the sum total of creation but the organized and responsible world"
  • Brown (The Gospel According to John) — counts 78 of the New Testament's 185 occurrences in the Gospel of John, and holds the world there oriented toward evil and dominated by it rather than evil in itself
  • Keener (The Gospel of John) — sets the universe sense aside as outside John's usage, and reads the command not to love the world beside God's love for it as one tension inside a single term
  • Michaels (WBC) — at 1 Peter 3:3 warns against confusing "the specialized use of κόσμος here as 'adornment'" with its commoner meaning of world
  • KJV, HCSB and NIV — divide at 1 Peter 3:3 between "adorning" and "beauty" for the one noun
  • NRSVue — turns that noun into a verb, "[d]o not adorn yourselves outwardly by braiding your hair", so the word itself drops out of the verse
  • John 1:10 — one verse using the word in three of its senses in succession
