CNT — Corey's Non-Translation
Theological: always untranslated Crux: untranslated here Variant: editions disagree

:

ἀποκριθεὶς δὲ ὁ Ἰησοῦς εἶπεν πρὸς αὐτόν Ἄφες ἄρτι, οὕτως γὰρ πρέπον ἐστὶν ἡμῖν πληρῶσαι πᾶσαν δικαιοσύνην τότε ἀφίησιν αὐτόν.

But having answered, Jesus said towarda himb, "Dimitc now, for thus it is fitting for us to pleried all rectitudee." Then he dimitsf him.

Notes

a
pros Every collated edition but one reads a preposition here rather than a plain dative pronoun. The construction stands nowhere else in Matthew, whose ordinary form after this verb is the dative, and it is the standard form in Luke. One commentary prints the dative against both standard critical editions for that reason. The same preposition closes the previous verse, which may have drawn it in.↑
Sources
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — print the dative against both standard hand editions, since the construction occurs nowhere else in Matthew while the dative form is attested at 15:15, 16:17, 19:27, 25:26 and 26:33
  • Willker (A Textual Commentary on the Greek Gospels) — rates the choice indecisive, counts the dative construction repeatedly in Matthew against this one instance of the preposition, and reports the preposition as the ordinary form in Luke
  • Weiss (Das Matthäus-Evangelium), cited in Willker (A Textual Commentary on the Greek Gospels) — judged the preposition "completely against the evangelist's usage"
  • Translators Amalgamated Greek New Testament — records the preposition in every collated edition except Westcott and Hort
  • Matthew 3:14 — the same preposition closes the previous verse
b
auton The competing reading is a dative pronoun with no preposition, and a further group of witnesses drops both words and puts nothing in their place. A papyrus from about 200 supports the accusative, while a sixth-century papyrus and Codex Vaticanus support the dative. The Latin has a dative where it usually renders the Greek preposition with a preposition of its own.↑
Sources
  • Willker (A Textual Commentary on the Greek Gospels) — sets out the papyrus support on each side, rates the choice indecisive, and reads the Latin as fitting the dative without settling it
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — record the witnesses that drop both words, and adopt the dative
  • Vulgate — reads dixit ei, a dative, where the preposition would ordinarily give dixit ad eum
  • Translators Amalgamated Greek New Testament — records the accusative in every collated edition except Westcott and Hort
  • Metzger (A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament) — his entry at this verse takes up the Old Latin tradition of a light on the Jordan rather than this reading
c
Aphes Two words in Greek, and the verb has no object. Of the seven singular aorist imperatives of this verb in Matthew, this is the only one followed by neither an object nor a verb of its own. Fifteen of the seventeen versions supply an object anyway. The lexicon files this occurrence under permitting, the same sense it assigns to "permit the children" at 19:14, which tells against the one version reading it as overriding an obstacle. One Greek verb covers sending away, letting go, leaving behind, permitting, abandoning, divorcing, and forgiving. English distributes these across several unrelated words, so a reader meets "forgive" in one verse and "leave" in another without seeing that the same word stands in both. The debt metaphor behind the forgiveness sense — a release from what is owed — is visible in Greek and invisible in English.↑
Usually left, forgiven, suffer
Sources
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — cites this verse under the "let, suffer, permit" sense
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — gloss the phrase "let it be so now" and call it "an idiom of permission", comparing the verb's pairing with the word for hindering at Mark 10:14
  • Hagner (WBC) — independently glosses the imperative "permit (it)", with the adverb "at once" or "immediately", and also "now" in a weaker sense
  • SBL Greek New Testament — the seven singular aorist imperatives in Matthew are 3:15, 5:24, 5:40, 6:12, 7:4, 8:22 and 27:49, and only this one stands without an object or a following verb; the three plurals at 13:30, 15:14 and 19:14 all carry one
  • Matthew 19:14 — the same sense of the same verb
  • Matthew 3:15 — the verb returns at the end of the verse, where it does take an object
d
plērōsai Two questions, not one. Why Jesus sought a baptism of repentance at all, and what "fulfil all righteousness" means. On the first, one commentary lists eight proposed answers and declines to choose. On the second, the argument is whether Matthew's righteousness-word is ethical or soteriological.↑
Usually fulfilled, filled, full
Sources
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — catalogue eight positions on the first question and conclude "[w]e remain in the dark", then adopt Meier's prophecy-fulfilment reading on the second
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — his own graded taxonomy, ranking repentance for personal sin least credible
  • Turner (BECNT) — rejects Bruner as anachronistic and Cullmann's atonement-typology as "overly subtle", favouring fulfilment plus solidarity with a repentant remnant
  • Przybylski (Righteousness in Matthew and His World of Thought) — the reference point for reading the noun as consistently ethical, rebutting Barth and Fiedler
  • Hagner (WBC) — the named dissenter, reading its primary reference as God's saving will
  • Boring (The New Interpreter's Bible) — reverses the "model for Christian baptism" reading, since the causal arrow runs the other way
  • France (NICNT) — an Isaiah 53 servant-echo, against Carson's objection that this reads Paul back into Matthew
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — against that whole line, calling a reading of the verse in terms of the passion "a widespread eisegesis of the text", and holding the righteousness here to be ethical
e
dikaiosunēn δικαιοσύνη, δίκαιος, and δικαιόω range in sense from personal moral righteousness to covenantal or forensic justice. Translators and commentators divide over which sense a given occurrence carries. No single English word covers that full range.↑
Usually righteousness
Sources
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — glosses δίκαιος "equitable (in character or act)," δικαιοσύνη "equity... specially (Christian) justification," spanning both senses
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — a two-tier entry, a broad sense "conformity to the Divine will in purpose, thought, and action" and a narrower sense "justice"
  • Przybylski (Righteousness in Matthew and His World of Thought) — argues Matthew's usage is uniformly ethical, "proper conduct before God," against a Pauline forensic-gift reading
  • Hagner (WBC) — an opposing view, that some occurrences (5:6, 6:33) denote eschatological "justice" or gift rather than ethical performance, explicitly against Przybylski
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — favors the "conduct" reading at 5:10, listing allied scholars (Barth, Fenton, Gundry, Luz, Przybylski, Strecker, Reumann, Ziesler) against dissenter Giesen
f
aphiēsin One Greek verb covers sending away, letting go, leaving behind, permitting, abandoning, divorcing, and forgiving. English distributes these across several unrelated words, so a reader meets "forgive" in one verse and "leave" in another without seeing that the same word stands in both. The debt metaphor behind the forgiveness sense — a release from what is owed — is visible in Greek and invisible in English.↑
Usually left, forgiven, suffer
Sources
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — one verb for sending away, leaving, permitting, divorcing and forgiving
  • Matthew 6:12 — the forgiveness sense used of debts, where the metaphor is release from what is owed
  • Matthew 19:14 — the permitting sense
  • Matthew 5:31 — the divorce sense
