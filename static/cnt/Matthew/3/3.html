CNT — Corey's Non-Translation
Theological: always untranslated Crux: untranslated here Variant: editions disagree

:

οὗτος γάρ ἐστιν ὁ ῥηθεὶς διὰ Ἠσαΐου τοῦ προφήτου λέγοντος· Φωνὴ βοῶντος ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ· Ἑτοιμάσατε τὴν ὁδὸν κυρίου εὐθείας ποιεῖτε τὰς τρίβους αὐτοῦ.

For this is the rhetheisa throughb Isaiah the prophet, saying, "Voice of one crying out in the wilderness: Prepare the way of kuriosc, straight make his paths."

Notes

a
rhētheis A masculine participle, occurring nowhere else in the New Testament. Matthew's ten fulfilment formulas all use the neuter instead, and all contain a verb of fulfilment, which this verse lacks. What that absence implies about John's standing is contested head-on.↑
Usually said, speak, tell
Sources
  • Gundry (Matthew: A Commentary on His Literary and Theological Art) — the missing verb is deliberate, and Matthew "keeps that verb for the story of Jesus", presenting John as a prototypical preacher rather than a fulfiller of Scripture
  • Carson (EBC) — names and rejects that reading, arguing the citation formula "can only be understood as identifying the Baptist in an eschatological, prophecy-and-fulfillment framework"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — with Gundry on the mechanism, that the absence is "likely to be because he reserves the formula quotations for the story of Jesus"
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — against that mechanism on the text's own evidence, since "[n]either 2.17 nor 3.3 has to do with the activity of Jesus: but both are directly related to his coming"
  • Turner (BECNT) — raises the same objection independently, that the reading "seems to be contradicted by 2:17", while siding with Carson on the outcome
  • France (NICNT) — with Carson, since John "is himself the subject of a prophetic oracle, not just the harbinger of the one predicted"
  • Hagner (WBC) — the formula here is a pesher-type one, "which points to fulfillment" despite lacking the characteristic verb
b
dia The critical editions read "through" where the Textus Receptus and the Byzantine tradition read "by". English versions divide between the two, and one carries the alternative in its margin. Matthew's citation formulas hold the prepositions apart, one naming the Lord as the source of what is spoken and the other the prophet through whom it comes (1:22, 2:15). The variant puts Isaiah in the place of the agent.↑
Sources
  • Wallace (Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics) — separates ultimate from intermediate agency, lists this verse among the passages naming a prophet as the intermediate agent, and holds that "the prophet's personality was involved in the shaping of its wording"
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — groups this verse with 2:5, 13:14 and 24:15 as introductory formulas close to the fulfilment citations without being one
  • Hagner (WBC) — tabulates the fulfilment citations, which use the same preposition for the named prophet throughout
  • Translators Amalgamated Greek New Testament — records the reading in NA28, NA27, the Tyndale House edition, SBL, Westcott and Hort and Tregelles, against the Textus Receptus and the Byzantine text
  • MacArthur Study Bible — prints "by" in its text and offers "through" in its margin
  • Metzger (A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament) — carries no entry for this verse
c
kuriou In Isaiah the word stands for the divine name, and the one whose way is prepared is God. The citation ends with a pronoun where the Septuagint of Isaiah 40:3 names God a second time, a wording Mark and Luke share. Whether the pronoun carries a claim about Jesus or merely avoids naming God is the dispute. κύριος ranges from an ordinary term of respectful address ("sir," "master") through a disciple's title for a living teacher to the full divine name. In the Septuagint it is the standard reverential substitute for the unpronounced Hebrew Tetragrammaton. When the New Testament quotes an Old Testament passage that names it, the Greek text carries the same word, κύριος, with no orthographic marker distinguishing it from the word's ordinary human sense.↑
Usually lord, master, sir
Sources
  • France (NICNT) — "there is a remarkable christological claim involved in applying Isaiah's depiction of God's forerunner to the man who prepared the way for the coming of Jesus"
  • Turner (BECNT) — ties it to Matthew's Immanuel christology, "the deity of Christ is implied here"
  • Boring (The New Interpreter's Bible) — independently, the substitution transfers Isaiah's prophecy of God's advent to Jesus
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — take the christological reading as primary, but raise avoidance of the divine name as an alternative, citing the Community Rule
  • Hagner (WBC) — argues the avoidance reading independently, without the Qumran parallel
  • Isaiah 40:3 (Septuagint) — the verse quoted, naming God where the citation puts a pronoun
