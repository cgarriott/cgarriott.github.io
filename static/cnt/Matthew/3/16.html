CNT — Corey's Non-Translation
Theological: always untranslated Crux: untranslated here Variant: editions disagree

:

βαπτισθεὶς δὲ ὁ Ἰησοῦς εὐθὺς ἀνέβη ἀπὸ τοῦ ὕδατος· καὶ ἰδοὺ ἠνεῴχθησαν οἱ οὐρανοί, καὶ εἶδεν πνεῦμα θεοῦ καταβαῖνον ὡσεὶ περιστερὰν ἐρχόμενον ἐπ’ αὐτόν·

Buta having been mersedb, Jesus immediately went up from the water; and behold, the heavens were opened, and he saw pneumac of theosd going down as a dove, coming upon him;

Notes

a
de The Byzantine tradition and the Textus Receptus open the verse with "and" before the participle, and place the adverb after the verb rather than before it. The critical editions instead put the conjunction second in the clause, after the participle, where it marks the turn from John back to Jesus. Older English versions carry a connective here, and the modern ones divide almost evenly over it.↑
Sources
  • Translators Amalgamated Greek New Testament — records the conjunction in NA28, NA27, the Tyndale House edition, SBL, Westcott and Hort and Tregelles, with the Textus Receptus and Byzantine text opening the verse with "and" instead
  • Willker (A Textual Commentary on the Greek Gospels) — collates the Byzantine form of the whole verse against the critical text, where the conjunction and the position of the adverb both differ
  • KJV — "And Jesus, when he was baptized", the older English form that follows the Byzantine reading
  • Vulgate — reads Baptizatus autem Jesus, a postpositive conjunction rather than an opening "and"
  • NET — prints the critical reading and reports that the conjunction has not been translated
  • Metzger (A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament) — carries no entry for this reading, though the verse holds two others he does take up
b
baptistheis Baptizo means to dip, plunge or immerse, and is used of ritual washing of both people and objects. English "baptize" is not a translation of it but the Greek word itself carried through Latin, so the English names a rite where the Greek describes an act. What the act physically involved — immersion, pouring, sprinkling — is disputed, and the word does not settle it.↑
Usually baptized, washed, baptist
Sources
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — dip, plunge, immerse, with ritual washing derived from it
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — separates ordinary washing from the initiatory rite
  • Josephus (Antiquities of the Jews) — describes John's washing as of the body, the soul already cleansed by righteousness
  • Taylor (The Immerser) — treats John's rite against the range of contemporary Jewish immersion practice
c
pneuma Pneuma spans a range from ordinary wind or breath to the animating principle in a person, an incorporeal spirit or demon, and the divine Spirit. Greek grammar does not resolve which sense is meant in a given case. The presence or absence of the article is sometimes read as marking the divine Spirit specifically, though this pattern is not applied consistently and remains disputed.↑
Usually spirit, ghost, wind
Sources
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — base sense "a current of air, i.e. breath (blast) or a breeze," extending by analogy to human soul, angel, demon, or God's own Spirit
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — five-sense entry, running from wind and breath through vital principle, disposition and incorporeal being to Holy Spirit, where the article's presence "as a rule" marks the Spirit as Person/Divine Power versus an anarthrous "operation, influence, or gift"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) and Davies and Allison (ICC) — both argue, independently, that "ἐκ πνεύματος ἁγίου" denotes divine creative power rather than a paternal/male generative role, Nolland via Septuagint idiom, Davies and Allison via grammatical gender (Greek πνεῦμα is neuter, Hebrew רוּחַ is feminine)
  • SBL Greek New Testament — πνεῦμα ἅγιον is anarthrous at Matthew 1:18, 1:20 and 3:11 but articular at 12:32 and 28:19
  • Blass, Debrunner and Funk (A Greek Grammar of the New Testament) — the article drops after a preposition and by assimilation to an anarthrous noun, which covers all three anarthrous occurrences
d
theou θεός ranges from a generic term for deity, one among many, to the proper-name-like designation of the God of Israel, usually marked by the article. An anarthrous predicate use is often read as qualitative, describing nature rather than identity, a distinction with no exact English equivalent. The word also applies, as in phrases like "son of God," to referents other than one unique divine being.↑
Usually god
Sources
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — "a deity, especially (with G3588 (ὁ)) the supreme Divinity", its own cross-reference there being to the article
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — distinguishes polytheistic use, anarthrous use (including as a qualitative predicate, "when the nature and character rather than the person of God is meant"), and the more frequent articular use
  • Harner ("Qualitative Anarthrous Predicate Nouns: Mark 15:39 and John 1:1," JBL) — argues anarthrous preverbal predicate θεός is typically qualitative, describing nature rather than identity
  • Colwell ("A Definite Rule for the Use of the Article in the Greek New Testament," JBL) — establishes that definite preverbal predicate nouns regularly drop the article in Koine Greek
  • Wallace (Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics) — refines Harner's rule, and treats passive-plus-predicate constructions of the they-will-be-called-sons shape as expressing a subset relation rather than strict identity
