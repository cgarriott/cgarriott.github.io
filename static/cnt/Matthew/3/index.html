Notes

a
baptistēs Baptizo means to dip, plunge or immerse, and is used of ritual washing of both people and objects. English "baptize" is not a translation of it but the Greek word itself carried through Latin, so the English names a rite where the Greek describes an act. What the act physically involved — immersion, pouring, sprinkling — is disputed, and the word does not settle it.↑
Usually baptist
Sources
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — dip, plunge, immerse, with ritual washing derived from it
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — separates ordinary washing from the initiatory rite
  • Josephus (Antiquities of the Jews) — describes John's washing as of the body, the soul already cleansed by righteousness
  • Taylor (The Immerser) — treats John's rite against the range of contemporary Jewish immersion practice
b
kai Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus omit the connective before the participle, and so do several early versions. The modern critical text prints it in brackets. Support for the omission is thin and does not fall along the usual textual lines. Versions that render a connective at all print "and saying" ahead of the imperative, and most modern ones open the sentence at the imperative instead.↑
Sources
  • Willker (A Textual Commentary on the Greek Gospels) — rates the choice indecisive, finds the support for omission slim and incoherent, and accepts the bracketing
  • Zahn, cited in Willker (A Textual Commentary on the Greek Gospels) — holds the connective is missed after the participle of proclaiming that precedes it
  • Translators Amalgamated Greek New Testament — records the word in NA28, NA27, the Tyndale House edition, SBL, Tregelles, the Textus Receptus and the Byzantine text, and absent from Westcott and Hort
  • NET — reports the Greek as "and saying" while dropping the following participle as redundant in English
  • Metzger (A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament) — carries no entry for this verse
c
Metanoeite A present imperative, a standing demand rather than a single act. Commentators converge on a comprehensive reorientation of life continuous with the prophetic call to turn, not a change of opinion. Where the word's "turn" force comes from is contested, since the Septuagint does not use it to render the Hebrew verb for turning. Metanoia is literally a change of mind. Classical and Septuagint usage runs from changing one's opinion to reversing a decision, without necessarily implying sorrow. English "repentance" imports moral remorse, and the Latin paenitentia behind it carried the further sense of doing penance. Whether the word demands grief for wrongdoing, or only a turn, is the question the English answers silently.↑
Usually repent, repented not
Sources
  • Meier (A Marginal Jew) — locates the turn-sense in first-century rabbinic tesuba rather than in Septuagint practice
  • France (NICNT) and Turner (BECNT) — both read a comprehensive reorientation, continuous with the prophets
  • CCSS — concurs, against a merely intellectual sense
  • Sanders (Jesus and Judaism) — a different objection, that the repentance-preaching belongs to John and not to the historical Jesus, citing Bultmann and Perrin
d
ēngiken A perfect tense, which describes a state resulting from a completed action. Whether that state is arrival or nearness is the whole question, and the versions divide three ways. The dispute has a long philological layer beneath the theological one, over what Hebrew and Aramaic verbs the Greek is rendering.↑
Usually drawn near, drew near, drawing near
Sources
  • France (NICNT) — the kingdom has arrived, not merely approached, citing Matthew's own sequence at 26:45-46
  • Gundry (Matthew: A Commentary on His Literary and Theological Art) — against France, nearness "right up to, but not including, the point of arrival"
  • Turner (BECNT) — sets out the schools, Weiss and Schweitzer for a strictly future kingdom, Dodd for a present one, Ladd for both
  • Hagner (WBC) — a middle position, "having drawn near and remaining near," citing Clark
  • Beasley-Murray (Jesus and the Kingdom of God) — traces Dodd against Campbell and Clark, and endorses Berkey's view that nearness and arrival overlap
e
basileia βασιλεία covers both the act of ruling and the territory ruled. Ninety-nine of its hundred and sixty-two New Testament occurrences take a genitive of God or of the heavens. Most commentators hold that the sense there is the ruling rather than the realm, and they divide over whether English "kingdom", now a concrete noun, should give way to "kingship", "reign" or "dominion".↑
Usually kingdom, kingship
Sources
  • Danker (A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament) — files the word under the act of ruling, and warns that "kingdom of God" is often understood as a royal realm with the reigning activity diluted out of it
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — makes "sovereignty, royal power, dominion" the proper sense and the territory ruled a metonymy from it
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — the classical entry runs the other way, leading with "kingdom, dominion" and giving the kingly office separately
  • France (NICNT) — "This dynamic sense is now better conveyed by an abstract noun such as 'kingship' or 'sovereignty' rather than by 'kingdom,' which has become in general usage a concrete noun"
  • Boring (The New Interpreter's Bible) — "'kingdom' is a verbal noun whose primary connotation is not territory but ruling power, 'reign' or 'rule' often being the best translation"
  • Marcus (Anchor), Stein (BECNT), Turner (BECNT) and Guelich (The Sermon on the Mount) — the ruling rather than the realm, Marcus printing "the dominion of God has come near" in his own translation
  • Nolland (NIGTC), Marshall (NIGTC) and Carter (Matthew and the Margins) — print "royal rule", "kingly rule" and "empire" in their own translations
  • Sanders (Jesus and Judaism) — keeps "kingdom", his charge of "evading the obvious by the semantic device of denying that by 'kingdom' Jesus meant 'kingdom'" aimed by his own footnote at the readings that make the word a symbol, not at the reign-not-realm rendering
  • Dodd (The Parables of the Kingdom) and Meier (A Marginal Jew) — with Sanders, retaining the word while granting what it suggests to a reader
  • Aalen ("'Reign' and 'House' in the Kingdom of God in the Gospels," NTS) and Ladd (JBL), cited in Danker — the standing dissent, a house or community of God and a realm, against the ruling sense
  • Dalman (The Words of Jesus), cited in Dodd — the Semitic noun behind it, "as related to God always means 'kingly rule' and never 'kingdom'"
f
ouranōn The phrase "kingdom of the heavens" occurs thirty-two times in Matthew and nowhere else in the New Testament, and this is the first. Mark and Luke write "kingdom of God" instead. Why the two differ is disputed six ways.↑
Usually heaven, air, sky
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — reverential avoidance of the divine name, which he reports as near-consensus, citing Howard
  • France (NICNT) — rejects that, since Matthew names God freely elsewhere, and proposes a bare stylistic preference
  • Carson (EBC) — rejects it on the same ground, then follows Pennington in reading heaven and earth as a sustained antithesis in Matthew
  • The Jewish Annotated New Testament — a third possibility, that Matthew views the kingdom as an actual place in the sky
  • Pamment — proposes that the phrase always denotes the future consummated kingdom, a reading Carson reports and rejects
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — finds no reason for the difference in Matthew's own theology, and traces the phrase to his community's liturgical usage
g
rhētheis A masculine participle, occurring nowhere else in the New Testament. Matthew's ten fulfilment formulas all use the neuter instead, and all contain a verb of fulfilment, which this verse lacks. What that absence implies about John's standing is contested head-on.↑
Usually said, speak, tell
Sources
  • Gundry (Matthew: A Commentary on His Literary and Theological Art) — the missing verb is deliberate, and Matthew "keeps that verb for the story of Jesus", presenting John as a prototypical preacher rather than a fulfiller of Scripture
  • Carson (EBC) — names and rejects that reading, arguing the citation formula "can only be understood as identifying the Baptist in an eschatological, prophecy-and-fulfillment framework"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — with Gundry on the mechanism, that the absence is "likely to be because he reserves the formula quotations for the story of Jesus"
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — against that mechanism on the text's own evidence, since "[n]either 2.17 nor 3.3 has to do with the activity of Jesus: but both are directly related to his coming"
  • Turner (BECNT) — raises the same objection independently, that the reading "seems to be contradicted by 2:17", while siding with Carson on the outcome
  • France (NICNT) — with Carson, since John "is himself the subject of a prophetic oracle, not just the harbinger of the one predicted"
  • Hagner (WBC) — the formula here is a pesher-type one, "which points to fulfillment" despite lacking the characteristic verb

dia The critical editions read "through" where the Textus Receptus and the Byzantine tradition read "by". English versions divide between the two, and one carries the alternative in its margin. Matthew's citation formulas hold the prepositions apart, one naming the Lord as the source of what is spoken and the other the prophet through whom it comes (1:22, 2:15). The variant puts Isaiah in the place of the agent.↑
Sources
  • Wallace (Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics) — separates ultimate from intermediate agency, lists this verse among the passages naming a prophet as the intermediate agent, and holds that "the prophet's personality was involved in the shaping of its wording"
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — groups this verse with 2:5, 13:14 and 24:15 as introductory formulas close to the fulfilment citations without being one
  • Hagner (WBC) — tabulates the fulfilment citations, which use the same preposition for the named prophet throughout
  • Translators Amalgamated Greek New Testament — records the reading in NA28, NA27, the Tyndale House edition, SBL, Westcott and Hort and Tregelles, against the Textus Receptus and the Byzantine text
  • MacArthur Study Bible — prints "by" in its text and offers "through" in its margin
  • Metzger (A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament) — carries no entry for this verse
i
kuriou In Isaiah the word stands for the divine name, and the one whose way is prepared is God. The citation ends with a pronoun where the Septuagint of Isaiah 40:3 names God a second time, a wording Mark and Luke share. Whether the pronoun carries a claim about Jesus or merely avoids naming God is the dispute. κύριος ranges from an ordinary term of respectful address ("sir," "master") through a disciple's title for a living teacher to the full divine name. In the Septuagint it is the standard reverential substitute for the unpronounced Hebrew Tetragrammaton. When the New Testament quotes an Old Testament passage that names it, the Greek text carries the same word, κύριος, with no orthographic marker distinguishing it from the word's ordinary human sense.↑
Usually lord, master, sir
Sources
  • France (NICNT) — "there is a remarkable christological claim involved in applying Isaiah's depiction of God's forerunner to the man who prepared the way for the coming of Jesus"
  • Turner (BECNT) — ties it to Matthew's Immanuel christology, "the deity of Christ is implied here"
  • Boring (The New Interpreter's Bible) — independently, the substitution transfers Isaiah's prophecy of God's advent to Jesus
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — take the christological reading as primary, but raise avoidance of the divine name as an alternative, citing the Community Rule
  • Hagner (WBC) — argues the avoidance reading independently, without the Qumran parallel
  • Isaiah 40:3 (Septuagint) — the verse quoted, naming God where the citation puts a pronoun

meli μέλι ἄγριον carries two senses in ancient usage. It can name honey from wild bees, or the sweet sap and gum exuded by trees, along with the drink made by mixing that sap with water. An ancient account of a desert people south of Judea applies the same adjective to a tree honey drunk with water. The adjective is classed at this verse with wild trees rather than with wild animals.↑
Usually honey
Sources
  • Kelhoffer (The Diet of John the Baptist) — assembles the ancient evidence for a "honey" that is tree sap, gum, or a drink made from it, and leaves the referent open, since "John's food is simply a reflection of what was plentiful in his midst"
  • Meier (A Marginal Jew) — takes neither side, the honey being either the sweet secretion of certain trees or the honey of wild bees
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — grants the plant reading may be correct, citing Diodorus Siculus, Pliny and Josephus, and records a patristic interpreter having to "expressly emphasize that the locusts have wings and that the honey is natural"
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — hold out against the plant reading, finding "insufficient cause for supposing 'wild honey' to be an exudation of certain trees"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — honey "presumably that produced in the wild without any role for a beekeeper", and a diet marking no rigid asceticism
  • France (NICNT) and Turner (BECNT) — the same bee reading, citing Judges 14:8-9 and 1 Samuel 14:25 for honey found in the wild
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — files this occurrence under the branch "of trees, opposed to ἥμερος", beside the wild vine
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — glosses the adjective "living in fields, wild" and cites this verse and Mark 1:6
  • Diodorus Siculus (Bibliotheca Historica), cited in Kelhoffer (The Diet of John the Baptist) — the Nabataean Arabs drink a tree honey his account calls wild
  • Vulgate — reads mel silvestre, honey of the woods, where the English versions print "wild honey"

potamōi Where the critical editions call the Jordan a river, the Byzantine tradition and the Textus Receptus print the place name alone. The English versions split the same way, and eleven of the seventeen carry the noun. Nothing in the sense turns on it, since the Jordan is a river under either reading. That John baptized in running water was later cited in the Christian argument over whether baptism had to take place in a river or stream.↑
Sources
  • Translators Amalgamated Greek New Testament — collates the noun in NA28, NA27, the Tyndale House edition, SBL, Westcott and Hort and Tregelles, and absent from the Textus Receptus and the Byzantine text
  • SBL Greek New Testament — prints the noun and marks the word as a point of variation
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — call it "strictly superfluous, and in the OT 'Jordan' usually stands on its own", and read the omission in the majority of the manuscripts as probably dogmatic
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — hold even so that it would be "unwise to read into the original text of Mt 3.6 anything about the evangelist's views on Christian baptism"
  • Didache, cited in Davies and Allison (ICC) — prefers that baptism be given in living, that is running, water
  • Orthodox Study Bible — prints "in the Jordan" without the noun, where Hart prints "in the River Jordan"
m
exomologoumenoi ἐξομολογέω sets ἐκ before ὁμολογέω. The lexicons give the compound both an admission of wrongdoing and an open acknowledgment, and record it in documentary formulae for admitting a debt. The participle is present, the form that ordinarily runs alongside its main verb. One reading holds the wording leaves the order of repentance, confession and baptism undefined. Five of the seventeen versions replace the participle with a temporal clause. Whether the confession was said aloud, and whether it followed a set form, is disputed.↑
Usually confess, praise, promised
Sources
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — anchors the confessing sense to this verse and the giving-praise sense to Matthew 11:25, and reads the compound's active as equivalent to the simple verb
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — records the compound in legal formulae for acknowledging receipt, in a first-century-BC papyrus, and used absolutely for acknowledging a liability, in a third-century-BC one
  • Danker (A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament) — separates an admission of wrongdoing from a distinct sense of open declaration, and compares the confession of the Community Rule at 1QS 1:24-26
  • France (NICNT) — holds the "participial wording leaves the link between baptism and confession undefined"
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — reads the confession of sins in this verse as evidence that forgiveness also takes place in John's baptism
  • Turner (BECNT) — "Perhaps the custom was for repentant baptismal candidates to confess their sins publicly as they were being baptized"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — reads the confession as made in the presence of God's representative, and treats whether any wider public heard it as of no significance
  • The Jewish Annotated New Testament — points the confession to Day of Atonement material at Tosefta Yoma 2:1, Jerusalem Talmud Yoma 8:9 and Leviticus Rabbah 3:3
n
hamartias The root sense is a missing of the mark, the failure of a thrown spear or arrow to hit its target. Classical usage extends this to any fault, failure, or error of judgment, then narrows in philosophical and religious writing to guilt or moral sin.↑
Usually sin
Sources
  • Danker (A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament) — three senses, an act of wrongdoing, "a state of being sinful, sinfulness" prominent in John, and "a destructive evil power" in Paul
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — the proper sense is "a missing the mark", the New Testament use always ethical, and the word "personified as a ruling principle" in Romans
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — the classical entry leads with "a failure, fault" and an "error of judgement", and reaches "guilt, sin" only "in Philos. and Religion"
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — derives the noun from the verb and glosses that verb "properly, to miss the mark (and so not share in the prize)"
  • Fitzmyer (Anchor) — the verb "retains in Paul's writings its basic meaning" of missing the mark, failing to attain a moral goal or standard
  • Moo (NICNT) — reads Paul as personifying sin from Romans 5 onward, so that acts of sin constitute a principle "so pervasive and dominant that the person's destiny is determined by those actions"
  • Grundmann, cited in Jewett (Hermeneia) — "For Paul sin does not consist only in the individual act. Sin is for him a state which embraces all humanity"
  • Röhser, cited in Jewett (Hermeneia) — the dissent, that Romans 3:9 and its context give no indication of such a personification
  • Moo (NICNT) — takes the prepositional phrase at Romans 8:3 as "sin offering", the sense it carries throughout the Septuagint
  • Septuagint — at Leviticus 4:24 the offering itself is named ἁμαρτία, the cultic use standing behind those alternative renderings
  • ESV and NIV — both footnote that cultic sense where their text prints "sin", the ESV "Or and as a sin offering" at Romans 8:3 and the NIV "Or be a sin offering" at 2 Corinthians 5:21

epi to baptisma Matthew writes that the Pharisees and Sadducees came to the baptism rather than that they were baptized, as the crowds were a verse earlier. Whether they came seeking it or came to watch is contested, and the question reaches published English versions, which divide between coming for baptism and coming to the place of his baptism.↑
Sources
  • France (NICNT) — the wording signals surveillance, and the reception John gives them "makes it unlikely that any of them actually were baptized"
  • Gundry (Matthew: A Commentary on His Literary and Theological Art) — anticipates the same reading by twenty-five years, "presumably for critical observation"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — against it, on discourse grounds, since "if baptism is not being sought, then there is nothing to link John's question to"
  • Taylor (The Immerser) — against the inference that rebuke means rejection, "John did not send them away"
  • Hagner (WBC) — a fourth position, that the motives were mixed
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — raise the surveillance reading without committing, and concede the rebuke fits sincere seekers better
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — reads the pairing as Matthew's construction, so that John and Jesus "face a common front of Jewish leaders," a literary purpose distinct from any claim about their actual motive for coming

Patera The word is fronted and carries no article, which makes it the predicate, as father we have Abraham. What John is attacking is disputed. The merits of the fathers, a doctrine by which the patriarchs' standing avails for their descendants, is the usual answer, and it has been corrected in three directions.↑
Usually father, parents, patriarchs
Sources
  • The Jewish Annotated New Testament — proposes the merits-of-the-fathers background
  • Keener (A Commentary on the Gospel of Matthew) — calibrates it, since the Abraham-specific form of that doctrine is attested mainly in rabbinic material long after this period
  • Taylor (The Immerser) — corrects it further, since the inherited merit is not biological, so the target is corporate standing rather than bloodline
  • Beasley-Murray (Baptism in the New Testament) — broadens it to the popular belief that Jewish identity alone secures the kingdom, citing Justin Martyr
  • Kraeling (John the Baptist) — reads the rebuke as a positive demand to match Abraham's own piety
t
theos θεός ranges from a generic term for deity, one among many, to the proper-name-like designation of the God of Israel, usually marked by the article. An anarthrous predicate use is often read as qualitative, describing nature rather than identity, a distinction with no exact English equivalent. The word also applies, as in phrases like "son of God," to referents other than one unique divine being.↑
Usually god
Sources
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — "a deity, especially (with G3588 (ὁ)) the supreme Divinity", its own cross-reference there being to the article
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — distinguishes polytheistic use, anarthrous use (including as a qualitative predicate, "when the nature and character rather than the person of God is meant"), and the more frequent articular use
  • Harner ("Qualitative Anarthrous Predicate Nouns: Mark 15:39 and John 1:1," JBL) — argues anarthrous preverbal predicate θεός is typically qualitative, describing nature rather than identity
  • Colwell ("A Definite Rule for the Use of the Article in the Greek New Testament," JBL) — establishes that definite preverbal predicate nouns regularly drop the article in Koine Greek
  • Wallace (Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics) — refines Harner's rule, and treats passive-plus-predicate constructions of the they-will-be-called-sons shape as expressing a subset relation rather than strict identity

tekna Matthew writes "children", not "sons". Behind the saying lies a Semitic wordplay, since the Hebrew words for children and stones differ by one letter. What the underlying form was is a live question, and the Aramaic does not carry the pun as cleanly as the Hebrew.↑
Usually child, son
Sources
  • Albright and Mann (Anchor) — state the Hebrew wordplay flatly, banim against abanim
  • Turner (BECNT) — the most cautious, since Aramaic uses a different word for son, so the pun would work only for those familiar with both languages
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — endorse Jeremias's proposal of a different Aramaic word behind "stones", and credit Chrysostom with the Isaiah 51 background
  • Charette (The Theme of Recompense in Matthew's Gospel) — argues against Jeremias, since the Hebrew pun is the more direct explanation
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — raises a separate question, whether the stones become children or bear them

  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — gives the verb as used for the passive of τίθημι

agapētos The adjective sits in the emphatic construction that repeats the article, giving it near-appositional weight. It may also carry a sense of "only", since the Septuagint uses it for Isaac at Genesis 22:2 where the Hebrew says "only son", and not with the dedicated Greek word for "only" that the same translation tradition had available. Where that sense comes from, and whether the Isaac connection is the right source at all, is disputed four ways. Agape is often said to name a distinctively divine or selfless love, set against philia and eros. The Septuagint does not support the distinction cleanly: it uses the same verb for ordinary affection and for Amnon's desire for Tamar in 2 Samuel 13. Whether the word carries any special quality of its own, or takes its colour entirely from context, is disputed.↑
Usually beloved, dearly beloved, dear
Sources
  • France (NICNT) — derives the sense directly from Genesis 22:2, treating the Akedah as one of two texts needed
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — the same conclusion by another route, from general Septuagint usage rather than a direct echo
  • Gundry (Matthew: A Commentary on His Literary and Theological Art) — a rival source, the Targum's paraphrase of Psalm 2:7, since Isaac typology plays little role elsewhere in the Gospels
  • Taylor (The Immerser) — a different background entirely, the word as a designation for a prophet, on the model of Daniel
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — the classical entry glosses it "of only children"
