CNT — Corey's Non-Translation
Theological: always untranslated Crux: untranslated here Variant: editions disagree

:

καὶ μὴ δόξητε λέγειν ἐν ἑαυτοῖς· Πατέρα ἔχομεν τὸν Ἀβραάμ, λέγω γὰρ ὑμῖν ὅτι δύναται ὁ θεὸς ἐκ τῶν λίθων τούτων ἐγεῖραι τέκνα τῷ Ἀβραάμ.

And do not think to say in yourselves, "Patritya we have — Abraham"; for I say to you that is able the theosb out of these stones to raise teknac to Abraham.

Notes

a
Patera The word is fronted and carries no article, which makes it the predicate, as father we have Abraham. What John is attacking is disputed. The merits of the fathers, a doctrine by which the patriarchs' standing avails for their descendants, is the usual answer, and it has been corrected in three directions.↑
Usually father, parents, patriarchs
Sources
  • The Jewish Annotated New Testament — proposes the merits-of-the-fathers background
  • Keener (A Commentary on the Gospel of Matthew) — calibrates it, since the Abraham-specific form of that doctrine is attested mainly in rabbinic material long after this period
  • Taylor (The Immerser) — corrects it further, since the inherited merit is not biological, so the target is corporate standing rather than bloodline
  • Beasley-Murray (Baptism in the New Testament) — broadens it to the popular belief that Jewish identity alone secures the kingdom, citing Justin Martyr
  • Kraeling (John the Baptist) — reads the rebuke as a positive demand to match Abraham's own piety
b
theos θεός ranges from a generic term for deity, one among many, to the proper-name-like designation of the God of Israel, usually marked by the article. An anarthrous predicate use is often read as qualitative, describing nature rather than identity, a distinction with no exact English equivalent. The word also applies, as in phrases like "son of God," to referents other than one unique divine being.↑
Usually god
Sources
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — "a deity, especially (with G3588 (ὁ)) the supreme Divinity", its own cross-reference there being to the article
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — distinguishes polytheistic use, anarthrous use (including as a qualitative predicate, "when the nature and character rather than the person of God is meant"), and the more frequent articular use
  • Harner ("Qualitative Anarthrous Predicate Nouns: Mark 15:39 and John 1:1," JBL) — argues anarthrous preverbal predicate θεός is typically qualitative, describing nature rather than identity
  • Colwell ("A Definite Rule for the Use of the Article in the Greek New Testament," JBL) — establishes that definite preverbal predicate nouns regularly drop the article in Koine Greek
  • Wallace (Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics) — refines Harner's rule, and treats passive-plus-predicate constructions of the they-will-be-called-sons shape as expressing a subset relation rather than strict identity
c
tekna Matthew writes "children", not "sons". Behind the saying lies a Semitic wordplay, since the Hebrew words for children and stones differ by one letter. What the underlying form was is a live question, and the Aramaic does not carry the pun as cleanly as the Hebrew.↑
Usually child, son
Sources
  • Albright and Mann (Anchor) — state the Hebrew wordplay flatly, banim against abanim
  • Turner (BECNT) — the most cautious, since Aramaic uses a different word for son, so the pun would work only for those familiar with both languages
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — endorse Jeremias's proposal of a different Aramaic word behind "stones", and credit Chrysostom with the Isaiah 51 background
  • Charette (The Theme of Recompense in Matthew's Gospel) — argues against Jeremias, since the Hebrew pun is the more direct explanation
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — raises a separate question, whether the stones become children or bear them
