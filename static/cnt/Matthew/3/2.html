CNT — Corey's Non-Translation
Theological: always untranslated Crux: untranslated here Variant: editions disagree

:

καὶ λέγων· Μετανοεῖτε ἤγγικεν γὰρ ἡ βασιλεία τῶν οὐρανῶν

anda saying, "Transmentb, for has engiziedc the basileiad of the ouranoie."

Notes

a
kai Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus omit the connective before the participle, and so do several early versions. The modern critical text prints it in brackets. Support for the omission is thin and does not fall along the usual textual lines. Versions that render a connective at all print "and saying" ahead of the imperative, and most modern ones open the sentence at the imperative instead.↑
Sources
  • Willker (A Textual Commentary on the Greek Gospels) — rates the choice indecisive, finds the support for omission slim and incoherent, and accepts the bracketing
  • Zahn, cited in Willker (A Textual Commentary on the Greek Gospels) — holds the connective is missed after the participle of proclaiming that precedes it
  • Translators Amalgamated Greek New Testament — records the word in NA28, NA27, the Tyndale House edition, SBL, Tregelles, the Textus Receptus and the Byzantine text, and absent from Westcott and Hort
  • NET — reports the Greek as "and saying" while dropping the following participle as redundant in English
  • Metzger (A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament) — carries no entry for this verse
b
Metanoeite A present imperative, a standing demand rather than a single act. Commentators converge on a comprehensive reorientation of life continuous with the prophetic call to turn, not a change of opinion. Where the word's "turn" force comes from is contested, since the Septuagint does not use it to render the Hebrew verb for turning. Metanoia is literally a change of mind. Classical and Septuagint usage runs from changing one's opinion to reversing a decision, without necessarily implying sorrow. English "repentance" imports moral remorse, and the Latin paenitentia behind it carried the further sense of doing penance. Whether the word demands grief for wrongdoing, or only a turn, is the question the English answers silently.↑
Usually repent, repented not
Sources
  • Meier (A Marginal Jew) — locates the turn-sense in first-century rabbinic tesuba rather than in Septuagint practice
  • France (NICNT) and Turner (BECNT) — both read a comprehensive reorientation, continuous with the prophets
  • CCSS — concurs, against a merely intellectual sense
  • Sanders (Jesus and Judaism) — a different objection, that the repentance-preaching belongs to John and not to the historical Jesus, citing Bultmann and Perrin
c
ēngiken A perfect tense, which describes a state resulting from a completed action. Whether that state is arrival or nearness is the whole question, and the versions divide three ways. The dispute has a long philological layer beneath the theological one, over what Hebrew and Aramaic verbs the Greek is rendering.↑
Usually drawn near, drew near, drawing near
Sources
  • France (NICNT) — the kingdom has arrived, not merely approached, citing Matthew's own sequence at 26:45-46
  • Gundry (Matthew: A Commentary on His Literary and Theological Art) — against France, nearness "right up to, but not including, the point of arrival"
  • Turner (BECNT) — sets out the schools, Weiss and Schweitzer for a strictly future kingdom, Dodd for a present one, Ladd for both
  • Hagner (WBC) — a middle position, "having drawn near and remaining near," citing Clark
  • Beasley-Murray (Jesus and the Kingdom of God) — traces Dodd against Campbell and Clark, and endorses Berkey's view that nearness and arrival overlap
d
basileia βασιλεία covers both the act of ruling and the territory ruled. Ninety-nine of its hundred and sixty-two New Testament occurrences take a genitive of God or of the heavens. Most commentators hold that the sense there is the ruling rather than the realm, and they divide over whether English "kingdom", now a concrete noun, should give way to "kingship", "reign" or "dominion".↑
Usually kingdom, kingship
Sources
  • Danker (A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament) — files the word under the act of ruling, and warns that "kingdom of God" is often understood as a royal realm with the reigning activity diluted out of it
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — makes "sovereignty, royal power, dominion" the proper sense and the territory ruled a metonymy from it
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — the classical entry runs the other way, leading with "kingdom, dominion" and giving the kingly office separately
  • France (NICNT) — "This dynamic sense is now better conveyed by an abstract noun such as 'kingship' or 'sovereignty' rather than by 'kingdom,' which has become in general usage a concrete noun"
  • Boring (The New Interpreter's Bible) — "'kingdom' is a verbal noun whose primary connotation is not territory but ruling power, 'reign' or 'rule' often being the best translation"
  • Marcus (Anchor), Stein (BECNT), Turner (BECNT) and Guelich (The Sermon on the Mount) — the ruling rather than the realm, Marcus printing "the dominion of God has come near" in his own translation
  • Nolland (NIGTC), Marshall (NIGTC) and Carter (Matthew and the Margins) — print "royal rule", "kingly rule" and "empire" in their own translations
  • Sanders (Jesus and Judaism) — keeps "kingdom", his charge of "evading the obvious by the semantic device of denying that by 'kingdom' Jesus meant 'kingdom'" aimed by his own footnote at the readings that make the word a symbol, not at the reign-not-realm rendering
  • Dodd (The Parables of the Kingdom) and Meier (A Marginal Jew) — with Sanders, retaining the word while granting what it suggests to a reader
  • Aalen ("'Reign' and 'House' in the Kingdom of God in the Gospels," NTS) and Ladd (JBL), cited in Danker — the standing dissent, a house or community of God and a realm, against the ruling sense
  • Dalman (The Words of Jesus), cited in Dodd — the Semitic noun behind it, "as related to God always means 'kingly rule' and never 'kingdom'"
e
ouranōn The phrase "kingdom of the heavens" occurs thirty-two times in Matthew and nowhere else in the New Testament, and this is the first. Mark and Luke write "kingdom of God" instead. Why the two differ is disputed six ways.↑
Usually heaven, air, sky
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — reverential avoidance of the divine name, which he reports as near-consensus, citing Howard
  • France (NICNT) — rejects that, since Matthew names God freely elsewhere, and proposes a bare stylistic preference
  • Carson (EBC) — rejects it on the same ground, then follows Pennington in reading heaven and earth as a sustained antithesis in Matthew
  • The Jewish Annotated New Testament — a third possibility, that Matthew views the kingdom as an actual place in the sky
  • Pamment — proposes that the phrase always denotes the future consummated kingdom, a reading Carson reports and rejects
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — finds no reason for the difference in Matthew's own theology, and traces the phrase to his community's liturgical usage
