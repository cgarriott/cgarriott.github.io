Theological: always untranslated Crux: untranslated here Variant: editions disagree

:

Ἐγὼ μὲν ὑμᾶς βαπτίζω ἐν ὕδατι εἰς μετάνοιαν ὁ δὲ ὀπίσω μου ἐρχόμενος ἰσχυρότερός μού ἐστιν, οὗ οὐκ εἰμὶ ἱκανὸς τὰ ὑποδήματα βαστάσαι αὐτὸς ὑμᾶς βαπτίσει ἐν πνεύματι ἁγίῳ καὶ πυρί

I on the one hand mersea you enb water into transmentationc; but the one behind me erchyingd is more ischurose than I, of whom I am not fit to bastazief the sandals; he himself will merseg you enh holy pneumai and firej.

Notes

a
baptizō Baptizo means to dip, plunge or immerse, and is used of ritual washing of both people and objects. English "baptize" is not a translation of it but the Greek word itself carried through Latin, so the English names a rite where the Greek describes an act. What the act physically involved — immersion, pouring, sprinkling — is disputed, and the word does not settle it.↑
Usually baptized, washed, baptist
Sources
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — dip, plunge, immerse, with ritual washing derived from it
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — separates ordinary washing from the initiatory rite
  • Josephus (Antiquities of the Jews) — describes John's washing as of the body, the soul already cleansed by righteousness
  • Taylor (The Immerser) — treats John's rite against the range of contemporary Jewish immersion practice
b
en The preposition can be locative or instrumental, and the versions divide: four print that John baptizes in water, twelve that he baptizes with it. The first makes water the element a person is put into, the second makes it the means John uses. The lexicons divide the same way.↑
Usually among, within, during
Sources
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — files this verse under the instrumental sense, crediting the Hebrew
  • Hart, Tyndale and the Douay-Rheims — print the locative "in water", following the Vulgate's in aqua, against the twelve versions that print "with"
  • Danker (A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament) — files the phrase the other way, as the external element the baptizing is done in, with the purpose following
  • Oliver ("Is βαπτίζω Used with ἐν and the Instrumental?," RevExp), cited in Danker (A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament) — a dedicated treatment of this construction
c
metanoian The preposition can mark purpose, cause, or reference. The commentators converge on reference, since the washing presupposes the change rather than producing it, which a first-century description of John's practice independently supports. Metanoia is literally a change of mind. Classical and Septuagint usage runs from changing one's opinion to reversing a decision, without necessarily implying sorrow. English "repentance" imports moral remorse, and the Latin paenitentia behind it carried the further sense of doing penance. Whether the word demands grief for wrongdoing, or only a turn, is the question the English answers silently.↑
Usually repentance
Sources
  • Carson (EBC) — weighs purpose, cause and reference, and favours "with reference to or in connection with", citing Blomberg and Harris
  • Hagner (WBC) — the identical conclusion independently, since "the baptism itself presupposes the existence of repentance"
  • Hill — proposes instead that the phrase subordinates John to Jesus, a reading Carson rebuts, since Mark and Luke describe John's baptism the same way
  • Josephus (Antiquities of the Jews) — the washing follows prior moral purification rather than producing it
d
erchomenos A participle used as a title, with future force. Who it names is disputed at least five ways, and the disagreement is explicit, with several scholars naming and rejecting each other's answers.↑
Usually come, went, came not
Sources
  • Hagner (WBC) — a technical term for the Messiah, and rejects Hughes's stronger claim that the coming one is God
  • Carson (EBC) — doubts any allusion to a fixed messianic title at all
  • Hartman (Into the Name of the Lord Jesus) — argues the phrase stood for God, and catalogues a three-way split among Vielhauer, Becker and Böcher
  • Meier (A Marginal Jew) — rebuts the whole God reading, since "a metaphor presenting John untying God's shoelaces seems to go beyond the bounds of any OT example", and argues for deliberate vagueness
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — survey six positions and favour the Messiah, against Elijah returned
  • Kraeling (John the Baptist) — a Daniel 7 figure, reached independently
e
ischuroteros A comparative, marked by a bare genitive. The lexicons treat it as an ordinary adjective rather than a title, but the same word is applied to God in the Septuagint and its cognate to the Messiah in a first-century Jewish psalm, and what weight to give that is contested.↑
Usually strong, mighty, mightier
Sources
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — cites this verse as the type-example of ordinary comparative usage
  • Carson (EBC) — the term is applied to God at Jeremiah 32:18 and Isaiah 40:10, and its cognate to the Messiah in the Psalms of Solomon
  • Hagner (WBC) — supplies the same Psalms of Solomon citation independently
  • France (NICNT) — a maximalist reading, that since the prophets have God pouring out the Spirit, "in some remarkable sense, when Jesus comes, God comes"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — against that, since bestowal of the Spirit is not a normal part of Jewish messianic expectation
f
bastasai βαστάζω means to lift or carry, and occurs three times in Matthew. Here it stands where Mark, Luke and John all write a different verb, untying the strap of a sandal. Two lexicons gloss Matthew's verb in this verse as taking a sandal off instead. Commentators divide over whether the two images name one servile act or two, and over which of them the saying carried first.↑
Usually bear, carrying, bear them
Sources
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — files this verse under "to bear, to carry", and reserves the later sense "to take away" for Matthew 8:17 alone
  • Hagner (WBC) — reports two lexicons glossing the verb here as "to take off", against his own reading of the wording as "possibly Matthew's heightening of the contrast between John and Jesus"
  • France (NICNT) — takes one verb to cover both images, since it "often means 'take away,' and hence in this context 'remove'"
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — read it as carrying rather than untying, and judge that "this hardly changes the substance"
  • Keener (A Commentary on the Gospel of Matthew) — the two verbs "convey the same sense of servility, and hence communicate the same point despite the variation in wording", and may render one Aramaic verb
  • Lagrange (Évangile selon Saint Matthieu) — carrying is the humbler office, a slave's rather than a host's courtesy, and the earlier of the two wordings
  • Ewald (Die drei ersten Evangelien und die Apostelgeschichte) — the reverse, that untying is the earlier wording and carrying suits a servant rather than the relation of pupil and master
  • Bretscher, cited in Davies and Allison (ICC) — a third reading, that the sandals are John's own and he is not worthy to wear them in the coming one's presence
g

English "baptize" is not a translation of it but the Greek word itself carried through Latin, so the English names a rite where the Greek describes an act. What the act physically involved — immersion, pouring, sprinkling — is disputed, and the word does not settle it.↑

h
en One preposition governs the spirit and the fire together, with no article on either, and that fact is itself an argument. Some read it as evidence of a single baptism with two aspects. Others rebut that the argument holds for the noun but not for the verb, so the spirit and the fire remain two things. The fire of the verses on either side is judgment fire, which is the weight on the second reading.↑
Usually among, within, during
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — the single preposition with a compound object indicates "a single baptism with ostensibly two aspects"
  • Carson (EBC) — the same ground independently, that one preposition governing both "normally suggests a unified concept", citing Dunn
  • Nolland (NIGTC), following Webb — against both, since to baptize "with the Holy Spirit and with fire" is as naturally two distinct activities
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — with Nolland, reading the fire of this verse as the judgment fire of the verses either side of it, so the spirit and the fire are two baptisms rather than one
i
pneumati Pneuma spans a range from ordinary wind or breath to the animating principle in a person, an incorporeal spirit or demon, and the divine Spirit. Greek grammar does not resolve which sense is meant in a given case. The presence or absence of the article is sometimes read as marking the divine Spirit specifically, though this pattern is not applied consistently and remains disputed.↑
Usually spirit, ghost, wind
Sources
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary — base sense "a current of air, i.e. breath (blast) or a breeze," extending by analogy to human soul, angel, demon, or God's own Spirit
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — five-sense entry, running from wind and breath through vital principle, disposition and incorporeal being to Holy Spirit, where the article's presence "as a rule" marks the Spirit as Person/Divine Power versus an anarthrous "operation, influence, or gift"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) and Davies and Allison (ICC) — both argue, independently, that "ἐκ πνεύματος ἁγίου" denotes divine creative power rather than a paternal/male generative role, Nolland via Septuagint idiom, Davies and Allison via grammatical gender (Greek πνεῦμα is neuter, Hebrew רוּחַ is feminine)
  • SBL Greek New Testament — πνεῦμα ἅγιον is anarthrous at Matthew 1:18, 1:20 and 3:11 but articular at 12:32 and 28:19
  • Blass, Debrunner and Funk (A Greek Grammar of the New Testament) — the article drops after a preposition and by assimilation to an anarthrous noun, which covers all three anarthrous occurrences
j
kai puri The two closing words are absent from the Byzantine tradition. They stand in every other printed edition, the Textus Receptus included, and one modern version's margin tells its reader of the omission. The parallel wording at Luke 3:16 is stable across the whole tradition, so the difference is particular to Matthew. Without the phrase the coming one's baptism is of the Spirit alone, the form given at Mark 1:8, John 1:33 and the two recollections in Acts. With it, whether one baptism is promised or two becomes a question.↑
Sources
  • Translators Amalgamated Greek New Testament — the words stand in seven of the eight editions collated, the Byzantine text alone omitting them
  • Willker (A Textual Commentary on the Greek Gospels) — names the omitting witnesses and rates the shorter reading indecisive, with a slight lean toward the longer text as original
  • Metzger (A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament) — carries no entry for this verse
  • Orthodox Study Bible — its margin reads "M-Text omits and fire"
  • Meier (A Marginal Jew) — treats the phrase as a later addition, since "baptism with holy spirit and fire unbalances the logion"
  • Beasley-Murray (Baptism in the New Testament) — reports and rejects the reconstruction in which fire alone was original, doubting that the Old Syriac "has preserved the right reading of Mt. 3.11 against the otherwise universal manuscript tradition"
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — takes the fire here as annihilating judgment, so that "[t]he baptism by the Spirit then probably does not mean the same for him as the baptism by fire"
  • MacArthur Study Bible — reads the same fire as "a baptism of judgment upon the unrepentant", distinct from the Spirit-baptism of all believers
