Notes

a
magoi μάγος names two distinct, long-coexisting senses in Greek: a dignified Persian or Median priestly caste skilled in astrology and dream interpretation, and a disreputable sorcerer or charlatan. Both senses were already current by the classical period and remained current through the first century. The narrative gives no hint of suspicion toward these figures, favoring the dignified sense, and no lexicon records a sense of "king."↑
Usually magi, wise men, magician
Sources
  • Liddell and Scott (A Greek-English Lexicon) — splits μάγος into a Persian priestly-caste sense (Herodotus, Aristotle) and an "enchanter, impostor" sense (Sophocles, Plato)
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — the same split, citing Matthew 2:1 for the priestly sense and Acts 13:6, 8 for the sorcerer sense
  • Danker (A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament) — a third independent lexicon confirming the split, tracing the priestly sense to Herodotus and Josephus
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — "later developments do not displace earlier usages, but rather the various usages tend to coexist"
  • Sim ("The Magi: Gentiles or Jews?," HTS) — argues against the majority Gentile reading, rebutting the arguments for it one by one
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — reads Matthew's first readers as bringing a negative attitude toward "magicians" to the text, so the narrative's favorable portrayal reverses their expectation rather than choosing neutrally between two live senses
b
anatolēi ἀνατολή is the noun cognate to ἀνατέλλω, "to rise," a verb used of stars and the sun. The phrase can mean either "in the east," repeating the geographic claim already made at 2:1, or "at its rising," reporting an astronomical event.↑
Usually east, rising
Sources
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — calls the phrase "generally recognized" as a technical astronomical phrase for a star's rising, citing Homer, Plato, and Euripides
  • Blass, Debrunner and Funk (A Greek Grammar of the New Testament) — the often-cited rule that compass points take no article is a single unsupported sentence with no New Testament example
  • Molnar (The Star of Bethlehem: The Legacy of the Magi) — reads the phrase as technical astrological vocabulary for a star's heliacal rising
  • Blomberg (NAC) — raises a syntactic alternative, that the phrase may modify "we saw" rather than "star"
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — judges the astronomical sense more probable while granting "East" attested for the singular elsewhere, citing Blass, Debrunner and Funk §141(2)
c
proskunēsai This is the first of three uses of the verb in this pericope, repeated at 2:8 and 2:11. The construction matches ordinary court homage to a human ruler, the same one Herod uses for his own pretext six verses later. Matthew applies the identical grammatical construction elsewhere to undisputed worship of Jesus, so grammar alone cannot settle which sense is meant here. προσκυνέω denotes full prostration before a superior, a gesture Greek usage associated with subjects bowing before Persian kings. The same verb covers ordinary court homage to a human ruler and the cultic worship of a god. Koine grammar supplies no separate construction that reliably marks which sense is meant. Context alone decides between the two.↑
Usually worship, homage, kneeling down
Sources
  • Carson (EBC), citing Broadus — "worship" need not imply the magi recognized Jesus's divinity, and may simply mean "do homage"
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — the Septuagint regularly pairs "come" with "worship" as a single cultic action, the same construction the magi's own request uses
  • Mitch and Sri (CCSS) — reads the magi's homage as already substantively divine worship, without flagging the ambiguity other commentators debate
  • Hagner (WBC) — Matthew's readers know the fuller sense of what the magi are doing better than the magi themselves would have known
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — reads Matthew's use of the verb across the whole Gospel as reserved almost exclusively for responses to Jesus, favoring the worship reading here
d
etarachthē ταράσσω describes agitated disturbance, of which fear is one common cause rather than the whole sense. Herod's own history documents a pattern of eliminating perceived rivals, including his wife Mariamne and three of his own sons, on suspicion of threatening his throne. His alarm at a rival claim to kingship fits that documented pattern.↑
Usually troubled, agitating
Sources
  • Josephus (Antiquities of the Jews) — records Herod's execution of his wife Mariamne, two sons, and finally his son Antipater on suspicion of threatening his throne
  • Turner (BECNT) — reads Herod's reaction as anxiety over a threat to his own rule
  • France (NICNT) — Herod's alarm is "hardly surprising, and reflects the extraordinary paranoia of his defense of his throne in his latter years"
  • Albright and Mann (Anchor) — pair the fear with the disciples at Matthew 14:26, and alone infer that "the fear results from a lack of faith"
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — reads the dismay as Matthew's own "signal" pointing forward to the passion narrative's "king of the Jews," not a report of Herod's documented psychology
e
sunagagōn This verb recurs at several points in the Passion narrative for the same leadership assembling against Jesus (26:3, 57; 27:62), and shares a root with the Septuagint's wording at Psalm 2:2 for rulers assembling against the Lord's anointed. Here the verb is active, with Herod as the one gathering others. In the Passion-narrative recurrences and in the Psalm, the verb is passive, with no gathering agent named. Some readers also hear an echo of "synagogue," the cognate noun for the leadership's own institution. Others judge that echo an over-reading of an otherwise ordinary word for gathering.↑
Usually gathered together, gathered, assembled
Sources
  • Mitch and Sri (CCSS) — reads the verb as recalling Psalm 2, where "the rulers of this world will assemble against the Lord's anointed king"
  • Brown (The Birth of the Messiah) — independently confirms the same Psalm 2:2 echo, noting the verb's frequent Passion-narrative use for assembling against Jesus
  • Gundry (Matthew: A Commentary on His Literary and Theological Art) — reads the verb as also punning on "synagogue," the institution dominated by the chief priests and scribes
  • Hagner (WBC) — rejects the synagogue wordplay as reading too much into an otherwise neutral word
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — takes the gathering as the point at which the scribes, recognizing whom they are describing, "become Herod's accomplices"
f
christos Herod's question uses this word where the magi's earlier question used "king of the Jews." Within Matthew, "king of the Jews" occurs only in the mouths of non-Jewish speakers, both here in the birth narrative and later at the trial and crucifixion. "The Christ" is the term a Jewish-context speaker reaches for instead. This is also the first point in the Gospel where the bare title is spoken by a character rather than by the narrator, and the speaker is a king hostile to the child he is asking about. Χριστός is ambiguous between a title, the Anointed One or Messiah, and a fixed second name for Jesus. Scholars disagree on where the word tips from title into name, and some read it as doing both at once. The verb χρίω behind it means to anoint, so every anointing in the New Testament is the same word as the name. Χριστιανός is the partisan noun built on it, formed with a Latin suffix meaning someone's people, as the Herodians were Herod's. It occurs three times, and what it meant at Antioch is disputed.↑
Usually christ
Sources
  • France (NICNT) — "the Gentile way of saying what a Jew would mean by 'Messiah' (the term which Herod, who could hardly refer to someone else as 'king of the Jews,' substitutes in v. 4)"
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — corroborates the same Gentile-only use of "king of the Jews" elsewhere in the Gospel with first-century non-Christian attestations of the title in Josephus
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — treats "king of the Jews" as the evangelist's marker for a Gentile speaker, who underscores that the magi are Gentiles by "having them ask in v. 2 about the birthplace not of the king of Israel but of the 'king of the Jews'"
  • Turner (BECNT) — reads the question as equating "king of the Jews" with "the Messiah," a linkage Matthew repeats through the Passion narrative (26:63, 68; 27:11, 17, 22, 29, 37)
  • Albright and Mann (Anchor), cited in Davies and Allison (ICC) — a separate claim about the title's currency rather than about Matthew's own usage, defending "king of the Jews" as genuine first-century Jewish speech on the Hasmonean king Aristobulus I's use of it
  • Danker (A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament) — cites this verse as its example of the titular sense, distinct from the later use of the word as a personal name
g
gennatai Herod's question retains the present tense for a birth already narrated three verses earlier in the aorist. Grammars distinguish a futuristic present, used for a certain future event, from a gnomic present, stating a general timeless truth, though neither category is a precise fit for a verb of this kind with a definite subject. Several commentators nonetheless read the retained present as marking a settled fact of prophecy rather than a request for news of a recent event.↑
Usually begat, born, brought forth
Sources
  • France (NICNT) — reads the present tense as indicating "a standing truth of prophecy," Herod asking for a theological ruling rather than news
  • Meyer (Critical and Exegetical Hand-Book to the Gospel of Matthew) — reads the verb as "purely present," not future in sense
  • Bengel (Gnomon) — reads the present tense as according with the general expectation of the Messiah's coming
  • Wallace (Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics) — defines the futuristic and gnomic present categories, neither of which cleanly matches this verb on his own stated criteria
h
oudamōs elachistē Micah's own wording states that Bethlehem is too small to be counted among Judah's clans. Matthew's wording states the opposite, that Bethlehem is by no means least. Both the Hebrew and Greek text traditions of Micah agree with each other against Matthew at this point, and no manuscript tradition of Matthew varies at this word.↑
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — reads the negation as the point where "Bethlehem's theological significance has finally overcome its geographical insignificance"
  • France (NICNT) — reads Matthew's negative as drawn from Matthew's own reading of the fulfillment, not from a variant text of Micah
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — reads the change as restatement rather than reversal, since the result is much the same
  • Stendahl (The School of St. Matthew and Its Use of the Old Testament) — reads it as free rendering, while treating a possible underlying Hebrew misreading as a live alternative

hēgemosin The Septuagint reads Micah's underlying Hebrew word as "thousands," its frequent general sense, where most modern renderings take this occurrence as "clans." The same consonants, differently vowel-pointed, yield "chiefs" or "leaders," the sense Matthew's Greek reflects. Matthew elsewhere uses this same Greek word for Pilate.↑
Usually governor, rulers
Sources
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — finds "princes" suggests "members of the royal family in a manner not implied here" and "governors" a Roman provincial office
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — records the word as reflecting a misreading of the Hebrew consonants as "leaders"
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — the Hebrew consonants could be pointed to give "princes," which explains Matthew's reading, since he is working with the Hebrew
  • Gundry (Matthew: A Commentary on His Literary and Theological Art) — reads the word Matthew applies to Pilate eight times as making "Jesus Pilate's superior, too, the true governor of Judah"

hēgoumenos It shares a Greek root with the earlier word rendered "rulers" (hēgemosin), though the underlying Hebrew words are unrelated. Where it comes from is disputed. One reading takes it from the Septuagint's wording of 2 Samuel 5:2, where it names David's own role as king. Another takes it as Micah's own ruler-word, chosen for that resemblance.↑
Usually esteem, count, leading
Sources
  • France (NICNT) — reads the word as drawn from 2 Samuel 5:2 to connect the coming ruler to David's own accession to the throne
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — holds the participle is the equivalent of the Septuagint's own archonta in Micah, and was "chosen because of its resemblance to" hēgemosin
  • Gundry (Matthew: A Commentary on His Literary and Theological Art) — with Davies and Allison, reading it as "the reappearance of 'governor'" in an anarthrous participle, carrying forward what the earlier word established, and noting that "the LXX has a different word"
  • Hagner (WBC) — with Davies and Allison, a synonym for Micah's own ruler-word chosen to agree with the word rendered "rulers" in the line before
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — a third position, chiefly a rendering of the Hebrew's own participle, with any reinforcement from 2 Samuel left uncertain
  • Stendahl (The School of St. Matthew and Its Use of the Old Testament) — a fourth, the word generated by assimilation to the earlier one and serving as the link into the 2 Samuel clause
  • Luz (Hermeneia) — names only the final clause and "the land of Judah" as possibly Matthew's own, leaving the citation's remaining peculiarities, this word among them, as older than him
k
poimanei The clause carrying this verb is absent from Micah altogether and comes instead, almost word for word, from the Septuagint of 2 Samuel 5:2, where it is the Lord's own word to David at his accession. Eleven of the seventeen translations keep the pastoral image and six replace it with governing or ruling, which is the whole question the word raises: whether the coming ruler's work is described in terms of care or in terms of rule. This is the only occurrence of the verb anywhere in Matthew. The three later shepherd passages all use the cognate noun instead.↑
Usually shepherd, rule, feed
Sources
  • Ignatius Bible, Tyndale, KJV, the Douay-Rheims, Vulgate and Luther — the six that replace the image with governing, Ignatius and Tyndale printing "govern", the King James and the Douay-Rheims "rule", the Vulgate "qui regat populum meum Israël" and Luther "der über mein Volk Israel ein HERR sei"
  • Gundry (Matthew: A Commentary on His Literary and Theological Art) — the clause "comes from the Lord's promise in 2 Sam 5:2; 1 Chr 11:2 to make David the shepherd-king over Israel", replacing Micah's own ruler-clause
  • France (NICNT) — the clause "defines the caring rather than despotic role of this ideal king in contrast with Herod's reign"
  • Turner (BECNT) — a contrast of legitimacy rather than of style, between "the genuine Davidic shepherd of Israel and Herod, who with his religious leaders is a false shepherd"
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — the clause "can hardly avoid evoking the eschatological expectation of the ingathering of the twelve tribes of Israel", citing Ezekiel 34, Psalms of Solomon 17 and Mishnah Sanhedrin 10:3
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — a Mosaic resonance alongside the Davidic one, since Moses too is remembered as a shepherd at Isaiah 63:11 and in the Biblical Antiquities, which they read as fitting Matthew's Moses-Messiah typology

lathrai λάθρᾳ occurs four times in the New Testament, twice in Matthew, both times in the infancy narrative: here, and at 1:19, for Joseph's plan to divorce Mary without publicity. The word marks concealment from others, not a lowered voice. Herod's private summons of the magi stands apart from his public consultation of the chief priests and scribes one verse earlier.↑
Usually secretly, privily
Sources
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — reads the word as carrying sinister overtones, citing Job 31:27, Habakkuk 3:14 and 1 Maccabees 9:60, and links it forward to the passion narrative's own clandestine plotting
  • France (NICNT) — grounds the secrecy in Josephus, since "Herod had a liking for the use of undercover agents"
  • Hagner (WBC) — reads the secrecy as "designed to keep the Jews from warning the magi of Herod's treachery"
  • Carter (Matthew and the Margins) — reads Herod's turn to the magi as following his own advisers' superior information at 2:5-6, echoing Joseph before Pharaoh and Daniel before Nebuchadnezzar

ēkribōsen ἀκριβόω occurs exactly twice in the New Testament, both in Matthew 2 and both in the identical inflected form: here, and at 2:16, where Herod acts on what this verse reports him learning. The adverb ἀκριβῶς, from the same root, recurs in the very next verse. English renderings like "found out" or "searched carefully" lose the echo between the two.↑

chronon Most translations take chronon as the star's precise moment of first appearance, matching the birth of the child. The Greek also permits a different reading: the length of time the star remained visible to the magi. Which reading is correct bears on why 2:16's resulting age cutoff spans two years rather than naming a single date.↑
Usually time, while, season
Sources
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — reads chronon as possibly "pertaining to the star's duration" rather than to a single instant

  • Turner (BECNT) — takes the gap as settled travel time, so that "the magi evidently arrived about two years after the birth"

proskunēsō προσκυνήσω is aorist subjunctive, the ordinary mood for a purpose clause, and carries no grammatical marker of insincerity on its own. The narrative later states that Herod sought the child's destruction, not worship (2:13), and shows him reacting with rage once the magi fail to return (2:16). Of the thirteen occurrences of this verb in Matthew, this is the only one the narrative goes on to contradict. προσκυνέω denotes full prostration before a superior, a gesture Greek usage associated with subjects bowing before Persian kings. The same verb covers ordinary court homage to a human ruler and the cultic worship of a god. Koine grammar supplies no separate construction that reliably marks which sense is meant. Context alone decides between the two.↑

estathē The critical text reads estathē, aorist passive of histēmi, against the Byzantine and Textus Receptus estē, an aorist active form of the same verb. No translation renders the two readings differently, since both describe the star coming to a stop. Both forms appear in the same split in the Septuagint of Numbers 9:15 and 9:17, the first of the tabernacle being set up, the second of the wilderness cloud's halt over the place where Israel then encamped.↑

chrēmatisthentes The verb denotes technical language for a divine communication, not ordinary speech. Its passive form here names no agent, unlike Joseph's own three dream-visions, each of which identifies the angel of the Lord directly. Two of seven surveyed translations make the divine source explicit in the verb itself. The rest leave it to be inferred from the dream setting alone. Commentators disagree on whether the missing angel reflects an abbreviated retelling of a fuller source or whether the verb's own oracular register already supplies the missing agent by itself.↑

kuriou κύριος ranges from an ordinary term of respectful address ("sir," "master") through a disciple's title for a living teacher to the full divine name. In the Septuagint it is the standard reverential substitute for the unpronounced Hebrew Tetragrammaton. When the New Testament quotes an Old Testament passage that names it, the Greek text carries the same word, κύριος, with no orthographic marker distinguishing it from the word's ordinary human sense.↑
Usually lord, master, sir

Egertheis The participle plus imperative here reflects a Hebrew idiom pairing qum ("arise") with a following command, rather than describing a separate physical act of standing up. kuriou The Greek distinguishes two kinds of agency: "by" names God as the statement's ultimate source, and "through" names the prophet as the means by which it reached the text. This same pairing, with an unnamed prophet, appears only here and at Matthew 1:22 among Matthew's ten fulfillment citations. One reason proposed for the pairing is that the quoted words are themselves presented as God's own first-person speech, placing responsibility on the Lord as their ultimate speaker rather than on Hosea as their human source. κύριος ranges from an ordinary term of respectful address ("sir," "master") through a disciple's title for a living teacher to the full divine name.
