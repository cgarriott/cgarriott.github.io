Notes

a
magoi μάγος names two distinct, long-coexisting senses in Greek: a dignified Persian or Median priestly caste skilled in astrology and dream interpretation, and a disreputable sorcerer or charlatan. Both senses were already current by the classical period and remained current through the first century. The narrative gives no hint of suspicion toward these figures, favoring the dignified sense, and no lexicon records a sense of "king."↑
Usually magi, wise men, magician
Sources
  • LSJ — splits μάγος into a Persian priestly-caste sense (Herodotus, Aristotle) and an "enchanter, impostor" sense (Sophocles, Plato)
  • Abbott-Smith (Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — the same split, citing Matthew 2:1 for the priestly sense and Acts 13:6, 8 for the sorcerer sense
  • BDAG (Bauer-Danker) — a third independent lexicon confirming the split, tracing the priestly sense to Herodotus and Josephus
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — "later developments do not displace earlier usages, but rather the various usages tend to coexist"
  • David C. Sim, "The Magi: Gentiles or Jews?" (HTS Teologiese Studies 55.4, 1999) — the magi's precise identity remains genuinely undetermined by Matthew's own text
b
anatolēi ἀνατολή is the noun cognate to ἀνατέλλω, "to rise," a verb used of stars and the sun. The phrase can mean either "in the east," repeating the geographic claim already made at 2:1, or "at its rising," reporting an astronomical event. Three verses later the same phrase describes a star moving ahead of the travelers, something a fixed compass direction cannot do, favoring the astronomical reading.↑
Usually east, rising
Sources
  • Davies & Allison (ICC) — calls the phrase "generally recognized" as a technical astronomical phrase for a star's rising, citing Homer, Plato, and Euripides
  • Blass, Debrunner & Funk, A Greek Grammar of the New Testament §253(5) — the often-cited rule that compass points take no article turns out, on direct inspection, to be a single unsupported sentence with no New Testament example
  • Michael R. Molnar, The Star of Bethlehem: The Legacy of the Magi (Rutgers University Press, 1999) — reads the phrase as technical astrological vocabulary for a star's heliacal rising
  • Blomberg (NAC) — raises a syntactic alternative, that the phrase may modify "we saw" rather than "star"
c
proskunēsai This is the first of three uses of the verb in this pericope, repeated at 2:8 and 2:11. The construction matches ordinary court homage to a human ruler, the same one Herod uses for his own pretext three verses later. Matthew applies the identical grammatical construction elsewhere to undisputed worship of Jesus, so grammar alone cannot settle which sense is meant here.↑
Usually worship, worshipped, worshiped
Sources
  • Carson (EBC), citing Broadus — "worship" need not imply the magi recognized Jesus's divinity; it may simply mean "do homage"
  • Davies & Allison (ICC) — the Septuagint regularly pairs "come" with "worship" as a single cultic action, the same construction the magi's own request uses
  • Mitch & Sri (CCSS) — reads the magi's homage as already substantively divine worship, without flagging the ambiguity other commentators debate
  • Hagner (WBC) — Matthew's readers know the fuller sense of what the magi are doing better than the magi themselves would have known
d
etarachthē ταράσσω describes agitated disturbance, of which fear is one common cause rather than the whole sense. Herod's own history documents a pattern of eliminating perceived rivals, including his wife Mariamne and three of his own sons, on suspicion of threatening his throne. His alarm at a rival claim to kingship fits that documented pattern.↑
Usually troubled, troubling
Sources
  • Josephus, Antiquities of the Jews 15.222–36, 16.392–94, 17.182–87 — records Herod's execution of his wife Mariamne, two sons, and finally his son Antipater on suspicion of threatening his throne
  • Turner (BECNT) — reads Herod's reaction as anxiety over a threat to his own rule
  • France (NICNT) — reads the alarm as unsurprising given Herod's documented paranoia in his final years
  • Albright & Mann (Anchor) — an outlying reading, treating the fear as a lack-of-faith failure paralleling the disciples at Matthew 14:26
e
sunagagōn This verb recurs at several points in the Passion narrative for the same leadership assembling against Jesus (26:3, 57; 27:1, 17, 27, 62), and shares a root with the Septuagint's wording at Psalm 2:2 for rulers assembling against the Lord's anointed. Here the verb is active, with Herod as the one gathering others. In the Passion-narrative recurrences and in the Psalm, the verb is passive, with no gathering agent named. Some readers also hear an echo of "synagogue," the cognate noun for the leadership's own institution; others judge that echo an over-reading of an otherwise ordinary word for gathering.↑
Usually gathered together, gathered, gather
Sources
  • Curtis Mitch & Edward Sri (CCSS) — reads the verb as echoing Psalm 2:2's rulers "assembl[ing] against the Lord and his anointed"
  • Raymond E. Brown (The Birth of the Messiah) — independently confirms the same Psalm 2:2 echo, noting the verb's frequent Passion-narrative use for assembling against Jesus
  • Robert H. Gundry (Matthew: A Commentary) — reads the verb as also punning on "synagogue," the institution dominated by the chief priests and scribes
  • Donald A. Hagner (WBC) — rejects the synagogue wordplay as reading too much into an otherwise neutral word
f
christos Herod's question uses this word where the magi's earlier question used "king of the Jews." Within Matthew, "king of the Jews" occurs only in the mouths of non-Jewish speakers, both here in the birth narrative and later at the trial and crucifixion; "the Christ" is the term a Jewish-context speaker reaches for instead. This is also the first point in the Gospel where the bare title is spoken by a character rather than by the narrator, and the speaker is a king hostile to the child he is asking about.↑
Usually christ, jesus
Sources
  • R.T. France (NICNT) — "the Gentile way of saying what a Jew would mean by 'Messiah' (the term which Herod, who could hardly refer to someone else as 'king of the Jews,' substitutes in v. 4)"
  • W.D. Davies & D.C. Allison (ICC) — corroborates the same Gentile-only use of "king of the Jews" elsewhere in the Gospel with first-century non-Christian attestations of the title (Josephus, Ant. 14.36; 15.373; 16.311)
g
gennatai Herod's question retains the present tense for a birth already narrated two verses earlier in the aorist. Grammars distinguish a futuristic present, used for a certain future event, from a gnomic present, stating a general timeless truth, though neither category is a precise fit for a verb of this kind with a definite subject. Several commentators nonetheless read the retained present as marking a settled fact of prophecy rather than a request for news of a recent event.↑
Usually begat, born, begotten
Sources
  • R.T. France (NICNT) — reads the present tense as indicating "a standing truth of prophecy," Herod asking for a theological ruling rather than news
  • Meyer (Critical and Exegetical Hand-Book to the Gospel of Matthew) — reads the verb as "purely present," not future in sense
  • Bengel (Gnomon) — reads the present tense as according with the general expectation of the Messiah's coming
  • Daniel Wallace (Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics) — defines the futuristic and gnomic present categories, neither of which cleanly matches this verb on his own stated criteria
h
oudamōs elachistē Micah's own wording states that Bethlehem is too small to be counted among Judah's clans. Matthew's wording states the opposite, that Bethlehem is by no means least. Both the Hebrew and Greek text traditions of Micah agree with each other against Matthew at this point, and no manuscript tradition of Matthew varies at this word.↑
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — reads the negation as marking Bethlehem's theological significance overtaking its earlier geographical insignificance
  • France (NICNT) — reads Matthew's negative as drawn from Matthew's own reading of the fulfillment, not from a variant text of Micah
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — reads the change as restating largely the same substance as Micah's wording
  • Stendahl (School of St. Matthew) — reads it as free rendering, while treating a possible underlying Hebrew misreading as a live alternative
i
hēgemosin Micah's underlying Hebrew word here is usually read as "thousands," the sense the Septuagint follows. The same consonants, differently vowel-pointed, yield "chiefs" or "leaders," the sense Matthew's Greek reflects. Matthew elsewhere uses this same Greek word for Pilate.↑
Usually governor, rulers
Sources
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — notes that "princes" wrongly implies royal blood and "governors" wrongly implies Roman provincial administration
  • Abbott-Smith (lexicon) — records the word as reflecting a misreading of the Hebrew consonants as "leaders"
  • Davies & Allison (ICC) — reads the repointing as what makes the word available, with resemblance to the following word hēgoumenos shaping the final choice
  • Gundry (Matthew: A Commentary) — notes Matthew elsewhere applies this same Greek word to Pilate eight times in the passion narrative
j
hēgoumenos This word is drawn from the Septuagint's wording of 2 Samuel 5:2, where it names David's own role as king, not from Micah. It shares a Greek root with the earlier word rendered "rulers" (hēgemosin), though the underlying Hebrew words are unrelated.↑
Usually esteem, count, leading
Sources
  • France (NICNT) — reads the word as drawn from 2 Samuel 5:2 to connect the coming ruler to David's own accession to the throne
  • Davies & Allison (ICC) — reads the resemblance to hēgemosin as shaping the choice of this word over the Septuagint's own "archonta" in Micah
k
poimanei This verb is drawn from 2 Samuel 5:2, where it describes David's own charge to shepherd Israel. It is the only occurrence of this verb form anywhere in Matthew's Gospel. Three later passages (9:36, 25:32, 26:31) use the related noun "shepherd" instead.↑
Usually shepherd, rule, feed
Sources
  • France (NICNT) — reads the clause as defining a caring rather than despotic role for the coming king, against Herod's own reign
  • Turner (BECNT) — reads an implicit contrast between Jesus as a genuine Davidic shepherd and Herod as a false one
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — reads the clause as evoking the eschatological gathering of Israel's twelve tribes
l
lathrai λάθρᾳ occurs four times in the New Testament, twice in Matthew, both times in the infancy narrative: here, and at 1:19, for Joseph's plan to divorce Mary quietly. The word marks concealment from others, not mere quietness. Herod's private summons of the magi stands apart from his public consultation of the chief priests and scribes one verse earlier.↑
Usually secretly, privily
Sources
  • Davies & Allison (ICC) — reads the word as carrying sinister overtones (citing Job 31:27, Habakkuk 3:14, 1 Maccabees 9:60) and links it forward to the passion narrative's own clandestine plotting (26:3-5, 14-16, 47-50)
  • France (NICNT) — grounds Herod's secrecy in Josephus's record of his use of undercover agents (Ant. 15.366-67; 16.236)
  • Hagner (WBC) — reads the secrecy as aimed at keeping Jerusalem's population from warning the magi
  • Carter (Matthew and the Margins) — reads Herod's turn to the magi as following his own advisers' superior information at 2:5-6, echoing Joseph before Pharaoh and Daniel before Nebuchadnezzar
m
ēkribōsen ἀκριβόω occurs exactly twice in the New Testament, both in Matthew 2 and both in the identical inflected form: here, and at 2:16, where Herod acts on what this verse reports him learning. The adverb ἀκριβῶς, from the same root, recurs in the very next verse. English renderings like "found out" or "searched carefully" lose the echo between the two.↑
Usually diligently inquired
Sources
  • Brown (The Birth of the Messiah) — reads the verb as a technical term from astronomical observation, preparing for the two-year cutoff at 2:16
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — reads the verbal echo between 2:7 and 2:16 as compositional glue joining two originally separate source traditions
  • Pettem (Evangelical Quarterly 93.3, 2022) — argues the standard "precise birth-moment" reading rests on the wrong, Hellenistic rather than Babylonian, astrological background
  • Davies & Allison (ICC) — reads chronon as possibly denoting the star's span of visibility rather than a single instant, easing the tension with 2:16's two-year range
n
chronon Most translations take chronon as the star's precise moment of first appearance, matching the birth of the child. The Greek also permits a different reading: the length of time the star remained visible to the magi. Which reading is correct bears on why 2:16's resulting age cutoff spans two years rather than naming a single date.↑
Usually time, while, season
Sources
  • Davies & Allison (ICC) — reads chronon as possibly meaning the star's span of visibility rather than a single instant
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — reads the two-year cutoff as evidence Matthew has merged an originally separate Herod source with the magi tradition
  • Pettem (Evangelical Quarterly 93.3, 2022) — attributes the apparent mismatch to translators assuming the wrong astrological framework
  • Turner (BECNT) — reads the gap between the star's appearance and the magi's arrival as implying real travel time of about two years
o
exetasate ἐξετάζω carries a register of close examination or interrogation, stronger than ordinary "seek." The verb occurs only three times in the New Testament: here, at Matthew 10:11 for a search for a worthy host, and at John 21:12 for an interrogation the risen Christ's own disciples decline to make. The search Herod orders here and the search at 10:11 use the identical Greek word, one ending in betrayal and the other in welcome.↑
Usually ask, inquire, search
Sources
  • Abbott-Smith (Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — groups the verb with anakrinō ("interrogate") and eraunaō ("search out") rather than the ordinary zēteō ("seek")
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — connects its use here to its later use at 10:11 for the disciples' own search for a worthy host
  • LSJ — cites classical usage for reviewing troops, cross-examining witnesses, and assaying gold
p
proskunēsō προσκυνήσω is aorist subjunctive, the ordinary mood for a purpose clause, and carries no grammatical marker of insincerity on its own. The narrative later states that Herod sought the child's destruction, not worship (2:13), and shows him reacting with rage once the magi fail to return (2:16). Of the thirteen occurrences of this verb in Matthew, this is the only one the narrative goes on to contradict.↑
Usually worship, worshipped, worshiped
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — titles his own section "Herod's Deceptive Plot" and calls the magi "unwitting accomplices" in Herod's scheme
  • France (NICNT) — calls the statement Herod's "unconvincing declaration of intent"
  • Boring (The New Interpreter's Bible, vol. VIII) — reads this verse's request as Herod's original, narrower plan, later abandoned for the massacre once the magi's warning intervenes at 2:12
  • Benson and Gill, among the pre-critical commentators — name the statement outright as hypocrisy, professing a wish to worship while intending to kill
q
anatolēi The phrase recurs from 2:2, where translations split between the geographic sense, "in the east," and the technical astronomical sense, "at its rising." Each translation carries its earlier choice through unchanged here. This occurrence marks where the star was first seen, distinct from its present motion ahead of the magi.↑
Usually east, rising
Sources
  • Hart — renders the phrase "at its rising" at both occurrences
  • JANT — matches Hart, reading "at its rising"
  • NRSVue — renders "in the east" at both occurrences
  • HCSB — renders "in the east," matching MacArthur, Ignatius, and OSB
r
estathē The critical text reads estathē, aorist passive of histēmi, against the Byzantine and Textus Receptus estē, an aorist active form of the same verb. No translation renders the two readings differently, since both describe the star coming to a stop. Both forms appear in the same split in the Septuagint of Numbers 9:15 and 9:17, describing the wilderness cloud's own halt over the tabernacle.↑
Sources
  • STEPBible TAGNT collation — NA27, NA28, Tyndale House, SBL, WH, and Tregelles read estathē against TR and Byz's estē
  • Turner (BECNT) — reads estathē's aorist as marking the star's punctiliar arrival against proēgen's inceptive imperfect
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — reads the star's stopping as requiring a miraculously provided heavenly light rather than a purely natural phenomenon
s
echarēsan charan megalēn sphodra The Greek stacks four elements onto the magi's reaction: a verb of rejoicing, its own cognate noun as object, an intensifying adjective, and a further adverb. Translations split over how literally to carry the stack, from "rejoiced exceedingly with great joy" to a single collapsed predicate like "were overjoyed." The construction reproduces a Septuagint idiom, occurring in the same form at Isaiah 39:2 and Jonah 4:6.↑
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — identifies the cognate accusative, intensified by an adjective and further by an adverb, citing BDF and Wallace
  • Dalman (The Words of Jesus) — traces the construction to the Septuagint's rendering at Jonah 4:6
  • Davies & Allison (ICC) — adds 1 Kings 1:40 and Nehemiah 12:43 as further Septuagint parallels for the same idiom
  • France (NICNT) — calls the construction difficult to capture in any single English idiom, rendering it "they were absolutely delighted"
t
eidon The Textus Receptus alone reads "found" (εὗρον) where every other collated edition, including the Byzantine text, reads "saw" (εἶδον). The difference is a genuine change of wording, not a matter of spelling or word order. The apparatus rates it too minor to affect translation, and no surveyed English version renders it differently.↑
u
prosekunēsan This is the third and final use of the verb in the pericope, now paired with a prostration participle absent at 2:2 and 2:8. Whether that addition tips the sense toward worship is disputed. Some read the participle as redundant unless full worship is meant. Others read the identical pairing elsewhere in the New Testament for ordinary court supplication, including a servant begging a king for mercy at Matthew 18:26. One standard lexicon classifies this occurrence under homage to human superiors, grouping it with the same verb's use at Matthew 4:9 rather than with acts of divine worship.↑
Usually worship, worshipped, worshiped
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — reads the added prostration participle as redundant unless genuine worship is meant, continuing his position from 2:2
  • Mitch & Sri (CCSS) — reads the addition as continuing a pattern of prostration-plus-homage that elsewhere marks undisputed worship in Matthew
  • France (NICNT) — reads the identical phrase as ordinary Eastern court prostration, unchanged from his position at 2:2
  • Brown (The Birth of the Messiah) — proposes the verb's own sense shifts across the narrative, from royal homage at 2:2 toward something less stable by this verse
  • Abbott-Smith (Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — classifies this occurrence with homage to human superiors, alongside Matthew 4:9, not with acts of divine worship
v
chrēmatisthentes The verb denotes technical language for a divine communication, not ordinary speech. Its passive form here names no agent, unlike Joseph's own three dream-visions, each of which identifies the angel of the Lord directly. Two of seven surveyed translations make the divine source explicit in the verb itself. The rest leave it to be inferred from the dream setting alone. Commentators disagree on whether the missing angel reflects an abbreviated retelling of a fuller source or whether the verb's own oracular register already supplies the missing agent by itself.↑
Usually called, divinely instructed, warned of god
Sources
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — reads the missing angel as an abbreviated, source-derived retelling of the fuller pattern seen in Joseph's dreams
  • France (NICNT) — reads the verb itself as already carrying full divine-communication weight, with the missing angel reflecting the magi's own idiom as dream-interpreters
  • Brown (The Birth of the Messiah) — independently reaches Nolland's source-critical reading, decades earlier
  • Hagner (WBC) — sides with France's reading of the verb's own divine register
  • Carson (EBC) — proposes, tentatively, that the magi's warning came through comparing notes with an already-warned Joseph
w
kuriou κύριος ranges from an ordinary term of respectful address ("sir," "master") through a disciple's title for a living teacher to the full divine name. In the Septuagint it is the standard reverential substitute for the unpronounced Hebrew Tetragrammaton, YHWH. When the New Testament quotes an Old Testament passage naming YHWH, the Greek text carries the same word, κύριος, with no orthographic marker distinguishing it from the word's ordinary human sense.↑
Usually lord, master, sir
Sources
  • Abbott-Smith (Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — states κύριος is used "in LXX (subst.) chiefly for יהוה, also for בַּעַל, אָדוֹן," cataloguing the full range from genitive-of-possession ("master of the house") through vocative address ("sir") to the full divine title
  • Strong's Greek Dictionary, G2962 — "supreme in authority, i.e. controller; by implication, Master (as a respectful title)"
  • John Nolland (NIGTC) — cautions against over-reading an anarthrous κύριος as inherently a divine title, noting Psalm 110:1's "my lord" is ordinary court address to a king
  • R.T. France (NICNT) — on Matthew's citation of Isaiah 40:3, applying to Jesus a text whose κύριος names God himself constitutes "a remarkable christological claim"
  • Martin Hengel, "Sit at My Right Hand!" in Studies in Early Christology (T&T Clark, 1995) — traces how Psalm 110:1's double κύριος became the primary vehicle for the earliest church's confession of Jesus's exalted status
x
Egertheis The participle plus imperative here reflects a Hebrew idiom pairing qum ("arise") with a following command, rather than describing a separate physical act of standing up. Translations render it with near-synonyms such as "get up," "rise," "arise," and "awaken," with no real difference in sense among them. The same command recurs verbatim at 2:20, and the narrator's report of Joseph's compliance recurs verbatim at 2:14 and 2:21, bracketing the whole flight to Egypt and back.↑
Usually raised, risen, arise
Sources
  • Abbott-Smith (A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament) — identifies the construction as a Semitism paralleling Hebrew qum plus imperative, citing Dalman
  • Davies & Allison (ICC) — confirms the Semitism with a fuller catalogue of LXX and Second Temple parallels, including Genesis 13:17, Deuteronomy 17:8, and Joshua 1:2
  • Turner (BECNT) — names the verbatim repetition between 2:13/2:20 and 2:14/2:21 as ironic, since Herod's threat and Herod's death frame the same journey
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — independently notes the identical verbatim repetition
y
kuriou The Greek distinguishes two kinds of agency: "by" names God as the statement's ultimate source, and "through" names the prophet as the means by which it reached the text. This same pairing, with an unnamed prophet, appears only here and at Matthew 1:22 among Matthew's ten fulfillment citations. One reason proposed for the pairing is that the quoted words are themselves presented as God's own first-person speech, placing responsibility on the Lord as their ultimate speaker rather than on Hosea as their human source.↑
Usually lord, master, sir
Sources
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — reads the pairing as fitting because the quoted words are God's own speech, not the prophet's
  • France (NICNT) — notes Matthew names only Isaiah and Jeremiah among his cited prophets, leaving citations from the Book of the Twelve, including Hosea, anonymous as a matter of course
  • TAGNT (STEPBible Translators Amalgamated Greek New Testament) — confirms every collated edition agrees on the wording, with only the Byzantine and Textus Receptus traditions adding an article before "Lord," a variant with no effect on translation
z
plērōthēi "Fulfilled" translates the same verb used at Matthew 1:22, but the text cited here was never a prediction in its own setting. Hosea 11:1 recalls Israel's own past deliverance from Egypt, not a future event, and scholars have long disputed what "fulfilled" can mean under those conditions. Proposed answers range from a genuine misapplication of the verse to a deliberate historical parallel drawn without claiming Hosea predicted anything.↑
Usually fulfilled, filled, full
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — sorts the scholarly positions into three camps: Matthew erred, a hidden fuller sense was already present, and typological recapitulation
  • Kirk ("Conceptualising Fulfilment in Matthew," Tyndale Bulletin 59.1, 2008) — independently confirms the same three-way split through a largely different set of scholars
  • McCasland ("Matthew Twists the Scriptures," Journal of Biblical Literature 80, 1961) — argues Matthew misreads Hosea's past-tense reference as a future prophecy
  • Sailhamer ("Hosea 11:1 and Matthew 2:15," Westminster Theological Journal 63, 2001) — argues the messianic sense was already present in Hosea's own words
  • Stefański (Teologia i Człowiek 28.4, 2014) — reports that Jewish tradition read Hosea's words as concerning Israel's liberation, not as messianic
aa
huion "My son" translates the Hebrew text's wording exactly, not the Septuagint's, which instead reads "his children," a plural, third-person phrase. Hosea's own words refer to Israel as a nation rescued from Egypt, not to an individual future ruler. Scholars disagree about how much weight this singular, personal wording can bear at this early point in the Gospel, since a fuller declaration of Jesus's divine sonship comes only later, at the baptism.↑
Usually son, children, child
Sources
  • Stendahl (The School of St. Matthew and Its Use of the Old Testament) — shows Matthew's wording follows the Hebrew and Aquila's Greek translation against the Septuagint
  • Brown (The Birth of the Messiah) — argues the phrase functions as a low-key, indirect reference to divine sonship, not yet the Gospel's full declaration
  • JANT (The Jewish Annotated New Testament) — notes Hosea's own words originally referred to the people of Israel, not to an individual
  • Davies & Allison (ICC) — traces the citation's associative background to Numbers 24:8, itself reached through the star oracle of Numbers 24:17
ab
enepaichthē Full LSJ treats the passive form here as its own distinct sense, "to be deluded," apart from the active "mock at" sense the same verb carries elsewhere in Matthew. Six of seven surveyed translations render it "tricked," "outwitted," or "deceived." Hart alone keeps a mockery sense, "made a fool by." The same verb, in active voice, later names the soldiers' treatment of Jesus at the crucifixion (27:29, 31, 41).↑
Usually mocked, mock, mocking
Sources
  • Davies & Allison (ICC) — reads the sense here as "duped," citing Jeremiah 10:15 LXX

kuriou κύριος ranges from an ordinary term of respectful address ("sir," "master") through a disciple's title for a living teacher to the full divine name. In the Septuagint it is the standard reverential substitute for the unpronounced Hebrew Tetragrammaton, YHWH. psuchēn ψυχή ranges across at least three senses: bare biological life, an inner self as seat of will and desire, and a periphrasis for the whole person. In the Septuagint it is the standard rendering of Hebrew נֶפֶשׁ (nephesh), a term closer to "living being" than to a philosophically separable immortal soul. Nazōraios No Old Testament text says "he will be called a Nazorean." Proposed derivations include the Hebrew netser, "branch" (Isaiah 11:1); nazir, "one consecrated to God" (Judges 13), reached by way of Isaiah 4:3's "he will be called holy"; "watchmen" in Jeremiah 31:6; a "despised and rejected" theme tied to Nazareth's obscurity; and the plain sense of a person from Nazareth with no wordplay at all.
