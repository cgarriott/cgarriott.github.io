CNT — Corey's Non-Translation
Theological: always untranslated Crux: untranslated here Variant: editions disagree

:

καὶ ἦν ἐκεῖ ἕως τῆς τελευτῆς Ἡρῴδου· ἵνα πληρωθῇ τὸ ῥηθὲν ὑπὸ κυρίου διὰ τοῦ προφήτου λέγοντος· Ἐξ Αἰγύπτου ἐκάλεσα τὸν υἱόν μου.

And he was there until the death of Herod, so that what was spoken by kuriosa through the prophet might reach its pleromab, saying, "Out of Egypt I called my filiusc."

Notes

a
kuriou The Greek distinguishes two kinds of agency: "by" names God as the statement's ultimate source, and "through" names the prophet as the means by which it reached the text. This same pairing, with an unnamed prophet, appears only here and at Matthew 1:22 among Matthew's ten fulfillment citations. One reason proposed for the pairing is that the quoted words are themselves presented as God's own first-person speech, placing responsibility on the Lord as their ultimate speaker rather than on Hosea as their human source.↑
Usually lord, master, sir
Sources
  • Nolland (NIGTC) — reads "by the Lord" as fitting "a cited text that must be spoken by God ('my son') and not by the prophet"
  • France (NICNT) — notes Matthew names only Isaiah and Jeremiah among his cited prophets, leaving citations from the Book of the Twelve, including Hosea, anonymous as a matter of course
  • Translators Amalgamated Greek New Testament — confirms every collated edition agrees on the wording, with only the Byzantine and Textus Receptus traditions adding an article before "Lord," a variant with no effect on translation
b
plērōthēi "Fulfilled" translates the same verb used at Matthew 1:22, but the text cited here was never a prediction in its own setting. Hosea 11:1 recalls Israel's own past deliverance from Egypt, not a future event, and scholars have long disputed what "fulfilled" can mean under those conditions. Proposed answers range from a genuine misapplication of the verse to a deliberate historical parallel drawn without claiming Hosea predicted anything.↑
Usually fulfilled, filled, full
Sources
  • Turner (BECNT) — sorts the field into three camps, rejecting the first two for attributing "error to Matthew" and "inscrutable hermeneutics to the Holy Spirit", and preferring a typological approach
  • Kirk ("Conceptualising Fulfilment in Matthew," TynBul) — independently confirms the same three-way split through a largely different set of scholars
  • McCasland ("Matthew Twists the Scriptures," JBL) — argues Matthew misreads Hosea's past-tense reference as a future prophecy
  • Sailhamer ("Hosea 11:1 and Matthew 2:15," WTJ) — argues the messianic sense was already present in Hosea's own words
  • Stefański ("'Rise, take the child and his mother, flee to Egypt' (Matt 2:13). But Why to Egypt?," Teologia i Człowiek) — reports that Jewish tradition read Hosea's words as concerning Israel's liberation, not as messianic
c
huion "My son" translates the Hebrew text's wording exactly, not the Septuagint's, which instead reads "his children," a plural, third-person phrase. Hosea's own words refer to Israel as a nation rescued from Egypt, not to an individual future ruler. Scholars disagree about how much weight this singular, personal wording can bear at this early point in the Gospel, since a fuller declaration of Jesus's divine sonship comes only later, at the baptism.↑
Usually son, child, foal
Sources
  • Stendahl (The School of St. Matthew and Its Use of the Old Testament) — finds that Matthew, unlike the Septuagint, "renders an exact translation of the M. T., as does Aquila"
  • Brown (The Birth of the Messiah) — argues Matthew "keeps the reference to divine sonship in 2:15 indirect (through the prophet) and 'low-key'"
  • The Jewish Annotated New Testament — notes Hosea's own words originally referred to the people of Israel, not to an individual
  • Davies and Allison (ICC) — traces the citation's associative background to Numbers 24:8, itself reached through the star oracle of Numbers 24:17
